Forma Studio had won three regional interior design awards in eighteen months. Their brand — a proprietary display wordmark generated through a combination of AI tools and manual refinement — was distinctive and widely recognised among their clientele.

When they approached a specialist signage company to produce lettering for their third studio location, the conversation ended quickly. The file was rasterised. The signage company couldn't use it.

## The Typography Problem

Forma's wordmark was built on a display letterform with a compressed, high-contrast character: tall x-height, thin hairlines, and a distinctive ink trap geometry that made the mark immediately recognisable. The problem was entirely in the file structure, not the design.

<Callout type="warning" title="What Rasterised Typography Produces">
When AI tools generate letterforms, they render them as pixels, not as font curves. The result looks like a typeface but behaves like a photograph. Characters cannot be selected, spacing cannot be adjusted, and at sizes below the native render resolution, hairlines collapse or disappear entirely. At signage scale, the hairlines in a high-contrast wordmark become the most visible element — and they cannot reproduce correctly from a raster file.
</Callout>

We opened the source file — a 2048×2048 PNG — and identified four specific technical problems:

| Problem | Impact |
|---------|--------|
| Letterforms rasterised (not paths) | Cannot be used by signage or embossing vendors |
| Irregular kerning (especially R-M pair) | Optical irregularity visible above 48pt |
| Two characters partially merged | Cannot be reproduced at small sizes |
| Hairline elements below 2px in source | Will not reproduce in print below 24pt |

## The Reconstruction Process

Typography reconstruction requires a different approach from geometric mark rebuilding. Each letterform must be studied as a drawing problem — understanding what the designer intended, identifying where AI generation introduced distortions, and rebuilding accordingly.

**Stage 1 — Typeface identification**
The base typeface used for the AI generation was identified as a compressed display serif family. This gave us the underlying structural logic — proportions, stem widths, serif construction — to work from.

**Stage 2 — Character-by-character rebuild**
Each of Forma's six letterforms was rebuilt individually as a custom vector path. The approach was not to trace the raster but to draw each character from first principles, correcting the proportional inconsistencies introduced by the AI generation process.

The compressed R-M kerning pair required particular attention: the raster version had a 3px optical gap that read as slightly too close at display size. The vector reconstruction corrected this to mathematical optical spacing.

**Stage 3 — Hairline specification**
The hairline elements — serifs, crossbars, and ink traps — were specified at a minimum production weight of 0.4pt. This is the threshold below which offset printing at standard resolutions produces inconsistent results. The AI output had hairlines as thin as 0.1pt at standard viewing size.

**Stage 4 — Type specimen document**
A complete type specimen was delivered: proportions for every character, spacing values for the most common letterform pairs, minimum size specifications, and production notes for vendors.

The reconstructed wordmark was used for all three Forma Studio locations. The signage company produced the display lettering without issue.
