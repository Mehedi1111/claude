Bloom Skin had built a clean, minimal skincare brand over eighteen months. Their Midjourney-generated botanical mark — a delicate line drawing of a bloom — had been refined through multiple iterations to achieve exactly the tone of deep midnight blue that the brand was built around.

The problem emerged when a Sephora category buyer reviewed their wholesale application. The buyer had seen the brand in three places: the website, the Instagram feed, and the physical packaging sample. She noted that the logo appeared to be a different shade of blue in each.

She was right. It was.

## Four Vendors, Four Blues

Bloom's brand blue — created in Midjourney and used without formal colour documentation — had been interpreted differently by every vendor in their production chain.

| Channel | Vendor | Colour Interpretation |
|---------|--------|-----------------------|
| Website | Web developer (hex) | `#1B2F5E` — bright mid-blue |
| Instagram | Social team (screen RGB) | `#1A2D5A` — slightly purple |
| Retail packaging | Packaging printer (unmanaged CMYK) | `#1E3366` — noticeably darker |
| In-store display | POP display vendor (RGB monitor) | `#162844` — near-navy |

Four different blues. Same logo. Four different vendors, each doing their best without a specification to work from. The cumulative effect was a brand that looked inconsistent to anyone who encountered it across multiple touchpoints — exactly the kind of problem that makes a retail buyer hesitant.

<Callout type="warning" title="The Hidden Cost of Undocumented Colour">
Without a Pantone specification, every vendor who prints or displays your brand applies their own interpretation. Over time, across a growing vendor network, a single brand colour becomes four, six, or eight different shades. The solution is not to find the 'correct' vendor — it's to create a single source of truth that every vendor works from.
</Callout>

## The Colour Audit

We began by measuring the existing colour values from each channel — pulling the actual rendered values from screen, packaging, and print rather than working from assumed specifications.

The original Midjourney colour was `#1B2F5E`. This RGB value had no documented CMYK or Pantone equivalent. Each vendor had performed their own conversion with their own ICC profile.

Through a combination of spectrophotometer proofing and Pantone matching tests on the specific substrates Bloom used, we identified the correct cross-media specification:

- **Pantone:** 2965 C (coated) / 2965 U (uncoated)
- **CMYK:** C100 M75 Y10 K45
- **RGB:** R0 G42 B94
- **Hex:** `#002A5E`

Note that this differs from Midjourney's output: the correct specification is slightly deeper and cooler than the original `#1B2F5E`. This is the truest representation of the blue that works consistently across every production context.

## The Logo Cleanup

The Midjourney mark also required technical cleanup before the colour correction work could be documented:
- 8 open paths causing fill inconsistencies at small sizes
- 3 elements specified as strokes rather than outlined paths
- Typography component with inconsistent weight on upscaled elements

All three were corrected. The cleaned mark was rebuilt at clean path level with the new documented colour applied at every component level.

## The Vendor Standards Document

We delivered a two-page brand colour standards document alongside the corrected file set. The document specifies the Bloom blue in every colour system, with application notes for each production context — specifying which ICC profile to use for coated packaging versus uncoated tissue, and how to calibrate digital display for in-store use.

The document was distributed to all six of Bloom's production vendors. The Sephora wholesale account was approved three weeks after the corrected files were delivered.
