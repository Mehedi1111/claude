Questions to ask before hiring a branding agency matter more than most founders realise. The wrong agency doesn't just waste money — it produces brand work that doesn't fit your business, delivers files you can't use, and leaves you starting over six months later.

The good news: the right questions reveal the right agency quickly. Here are the ones worth asking.

---

## Do You Have Case Studies Where the Brand Is Still Active?

This is the most underused question in the industry.

A portfolio shows you what an agency can make. A case study where the brand is still being used — not replaced, not redesigned, not abandoned — shows you whether it worked.

Ask to see 3 examples. Ask what the client is doing today. Ask if you can contact the client. The answers tell you a great deal about whether this agency's work actually survives contact with the real world.

If the portfolio is full of concept work, personal projects, or rebrands that were quickly replaced, that's a signal worth taking seriously.

---

## Who Will Actually Do the Work?

Some agencies have a senior designer on their website who handles sales — and then hand your project to a junior designer you never meet.

Ask directly: who will be my main designer? Can I speak to them before we start? Will that person be doing most of the design work, or is my project going to be delegated?

There is nothing wrong with a junior designer doing good work. The problem is paying senior rates for junior work while being told otherwise.

A transparent agency answers this question without hesitation. [Knowing who will handle your project](/blog/how-to-brief-a-logo-designer) is also important when it comes to briefing — the person who takes the brief should be the person doing the work.

---

## What Do You Need From Me to Start?

A good agency will give you a real answer. Brand questionnaire. Competitor examples. Inspiration references. Current assets. Target audience details.

An agency that says "just send us your brief and we'll get started" is skipping discovery. You can't build a brand strategy without understanding the business. You can't design a meaningful logo without knowing who the audience is and what feeling it needs to trigger.

The more structured their onboarding, the more seriously they take getting the foundations right.

---

## How Many Concepts Will You Show Me?

This question matters because the answer affects what you get.

Some agencies show 1 concept and refine it. Others show 3 directions and let you choose. Neither approach is inherently wrong — but you need to know which one you're paying for.

"3 concepts" sounds better until you realise each one is a 20-minute sketch. "1 concept" sounds limiting until you learn it's 40 hours of focused strategic work. Ask what's included in each concept, not just how many there are.

---

## How Does the Revision Process Work?

Almost every agency includes "revisions" in their quote. Very few make clear what that means.

Ask: how many rounds of revisions are included? What counts as a revision — does changing a colour count the same as changing the entire concept? What happens if I need changes after the project closes?

Get this in writing before you sign anything. Revision scope is the most common source of conflict in branding projects. A clear revision policy upfront prevents a lot of friction later. For context on what a fair revision structure looks like, [the logo design process guide](/blog/logo-design-process) walks through how professional agencies typically structure feedback rounds.

---

## What Files Will I Receive at the End?

This question trips up a lot of first-time brand buyers.

You need: vector files (AI or EPS), SVG for web, PNG with transparent background, JPEG for documents. You need colour versions and mono versions. You need guidelines for how to use the logo correctly.

If an agency hands you a JPEG and calls it done, you don't own a usable brand. You own an image file. For a full breakdown of what to expect, [what files should a designer deliver](/blog/what-files-should-a-designer-deliver) covers exactly what a complete brand file delivery looks like.

Ask specifically: will I receive the original source files? Some agencies retain ownership of the source files and only license the logo to you — which limits what you can do with it later. Most reputable agencies transfer full ownership, but confirm it before you start.

---

## Do You Handle Brand Strategy or Just the Visual Design?

There's a big difference between a designer who makes logos and a brand strategist who builds brand identity systems.

If you need a logo, a good designer is enough. If you need a complete brand identity — positioning, messaging, visual system, guidelines — you need an agency that works at the strategic level, not just the visual one.

Ask what they include in their brand identity process. Does it include a brand strategy document? A competitor landscape review? Messaging and tone of voice guidance? Or does it go straight to logo concepts after a brief call?

Neither approach is wrong for every client. The key is knowing what you're buying. [Brand identity vs logo design](/blog/logo-design-vs-brand-identity) explains the distinction clearly if you're unsure which one your business actually needs right now.

---

## What Happens If I'm Not Happy With the Result?

Ask this directly. Watch how they respond.

A good agency has a clear answer: we offer 2 revision rounds to address strategic feedback, and if the project fundamentally isn't working after that, here's what we do. They've thought about this because it occasionally happens even with good work.

An agency that says "that won't happen" or gets defensive is telling you they haven't thought about it — or that their process for handling it is to argue with you.

---

## What Do You Need to Know About My Competitors?

This question reveals how strategic the agency thinks.

A surface-level agency will say "send us a few examples you like." A strategic agency will use competitor research to identify positioning gaps, visual conventions in your industry worth breaking, and territories you can own.

If they're not asking about your competitors before they design anything, they're designing in a vacuum. Brand work without competitive context is just decoration. Good brand positioning — which is what makes a brand actually valuable — comes from understanding what space is available for you to own in your category.

---

## Have You Worked With Businesses at My Stage?

A startup founder needing a launch identity has different requirements than an established firm rebranding after 15 years. The right agency for one is not always right for the other.

Ask if they have experience at your stage. Ask what that work typically involves. Some agencies thrive with early-stage businesses where speed and clarity matter most. Others do their best work with established brands where there's deep equity to work with. Neither is better — match matters.

---

## What's the Timeline, and What Could Delay It?

Every agency gives you a timeline. Almost none tell you what could make it longer.

Ask what the most common causes of delay are. The honest answer is usually: client feedback taking longer than expected, scope expanding after the project starts, or additional rounds of revisions beyond what was planned.

Knowing this upfront helps you move faster on your side when feedback is needed — and helps you build a realistic expectation of when the brand will actually be ready for use.

---

## Can You Show Me Work for a Business Similar to Mine?

A logo agency that's done great work for tech startups may struggle with a law firm. An agency that specialises in luxury brands may not understand what a service business with a $500 project needs.

Ask for relevant portfolio examples. Not just "here's our best work" — ask specifically for examples closest to your industry, audience, and price point. If they don't have any, that's not disqualifying — but it's worth understanding what adjustments they'll need to make.

---

The right agency answers these questions easily and specifically. They're not surprised by them. They've been asked before, they have clear answers, and they welcome the due diligence.

If asking these questions feels like you're being difficult, find a different agency.
