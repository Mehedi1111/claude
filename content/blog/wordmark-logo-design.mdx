Some of the most recognisable logos in the world have no symbol at all.

Google. Coca-Cola. FedEx. Visa. Netflix.

What makes them recognisable isn't an icon — it's the name itself, set in a typeface that's been designed or selected so deliberately that it becomes inseparable from the brand.

That's a wordmark. And when it's done well, it's one of the most powerful logo formats you can choose.

---

## What a Wordmark Logo Actually Is

A wordmark is a logo built entirely from typography — the full business name, nothing else.

No icon beside it. No symbol underneath it. No graphic element framing it. Just the name, rendered in a typeface that's been chosen or customised to carry the brand's character.

The power of a wordmark is direct: every time someone sees it, they see the name. There's no intermediary symbol to associate and then translate. The name is the logo. The logo is the name.

This directness is what makes wordmarks so effective for name recognition. Every impression builds awareness of the actual business name, not of a symbol that then needs to be connected to the name.

---

## Wordmark vs. Lettermark vs. Combination Mark

Logo types are often confused with each other. Here's how wordmarks differ from the two most similar alternatives.

**Wordmark vs. lettermark.** A wordmark spells out the full business name. A lettermark uses only the initials — think IBM, CNN, or HBO. The choice between them usually comes down to name length and whether the abbreviation is already established in the market. A two-word business name is usually a wordmark candidate. A five-word name is usually a lettermark candidate.

**Wordmark vs. combination mark.** A [combination mark](/blog/combination-mark-logo-design) pairs a symbol with a wordmark — the icon and the name together. This gives you flexibility: you can use the full combination mark in most contexts and the symbol alone in contexts where the brand is already established. A pure wordmark is simpler and more direct, but lacks a standalone icon for situations where one is needed (app icon, favicon, embroidered badge).

Choosing between these is a real strategic decision. It depends on the name's length, distinctiveness, and where the logo will be used most.

## When a Wordmark Works Best

A wordmark is the right choice in specific circumstances. It isn't always the answer.

**Your business name is distinctive and pronounceable.** A wordmark builds recognition of the name itself. If the name is generic, forgettable, or shared with other businesses, a wordmark can't do much work for you — there's nothing memorable to anchor to.

**Your name is the right length.** Wordmarks work best when the name is short enough to be readable at the sizes a logo actually gets used. One to three words, under 15–18 characters, is the sweet spot. "Google" works as a wordmark. "National Association of Professional Accountants" does not.

**You're building name recognition from scratch.** For a new business, every visual impression is a chance to build name recognition. A wordmark guarantees that every impression includes the name — there's no symbolic detour. This is why many startups and new professional services firms choose wordmarks.

**You work primarily in digital contexts.** Wordmarks are highly legible on websites, email signatures, and digital documents. They scale cleanly at all sizes. They work in dark mode and light mode. They don't lose detail the way complex symbols can at small sizes.

**You have a strong enough typographic identity.** A wordmark lives or dies by the quality of the typography. If the typeface is generic — a common system font with no customisation — the logo looks like placeholder text. The typography needs to carry the brand's character on its own.

---

## When a Wordmark Is the Wrong Choice

A wordmark is not always right. There are specific situations where another format serves the brand better.

**When the name is too long.** A five-word business name set in any typeface becomes an illegible string of letters at small logo sizes. It won't work as a profile photo, a favicon, an embroidered patch, or an app icon. Businesses with long names need either a lettermark or a combination mark where the icon handles small-size applications.

**When you need a symbol for icon use.** A wordmark has no standalone icon component. At 32×32 pixels — favicon size — a wordmark is unreadable. At 180×180 pixels — iPhone app icon size — a wordmark with a short name might work, but most don't. If a standalone icon is important for your use cases, a wordmark alone won't provide it.

**When the brand operates in a category where symbols carry specific meaning.** In healthcare, the cross. In finance, specific geometric marks. In some industries, visual symbols carry cultural or professional meaning that a wordmark can't replicate.

**When you need to work without a name.** Brands that operate in merchandise, global markets, or contexts where the name isn't visible benefit from having a recognisable symbol. A wordmark only works when people can read it. A symbol works in any language.

## How Wordmarks Are Designed

A good wordmark is not just the business name typed in a font.

At the basic level, choosing a typeface well is itself a significant design decision. Serif or sans-serif. Geometric or humanist. Display weight or regular. Each of these communicates something about the brand's character. The typeface needs to match what the brand is trying to say.

At the professional level, wordmarks are customised beyond the base typeface. Specific letters are redrawn. Spacing between letters is adjusted precisely. Specific characters — particularly the first letter or letters — may be significantly altered to create a distinctive mark that couldn't be replicated by just typing the name.

The most recognisable wordmarks have letter-level customisation that makes them unmistakably themselves. The Google "G" has been refined through many iterations. The Coca-Cola script has specific letterform connections that are entirely custom. The FedEx wordmark has hidden negative space in the letters.

This is why professional wordmarks require a designer — not just a font selection tool. The typeface is the starting point, not the ending point.

---

## Typography Choices That Define Wordmarks

The typeface category a wordmark uses says something specific about the brand.

**Serif wordmarks** communicate heritage, authority, and institutional stability. Law firms, financial institutions, media companies, and heritage brands often use serif wordmarks. The serifs themselves signal tradition and precision.

**Sans-serif wordmarks** communicate modernity, clarity, and approachability. Tech companies, startups, and brands targeting contemporary audiences typically use geometric or humanist sans-serifs. They feel clean and forward-looking.

**Script and handwritten wordmarks** communicate creativity, warmth, and personality. Luxury brands, food and beverage companies, and personal brands sometimes use script wordmarks. They feel handcrafted and personal — but only work well in limited size ranges and lose legibility at very small sizes.

**Custom lettering** is a wordmark where the letterforms are entirely purpose-built, not drawn from an existing typeface. These are the most distinctive wordmarks — there's no font file anyone can install to replicate the logo. They require the most investment but produce the most ownable result.

[How to choose logo fonts](/blog/how-to-choose-logo-fonts) covers the specific decisions involved in typeface selection for logo work. For a wordmark specifically, this decision carries even more weight than in other logo formats — the type is everything.

## File Requirements for Wordmark Logos

A professional wordmark logo delivery has the same file requirements as any other logo format.

**Vector source file (AI).** The original editable Illustrator file. Wordmarks must be delivered as outlined vector text — not live type. If the wordmark is delivered as live type, any machine without that font installed will substitute a different typeface, breaking the logo.

**EPS.** For print production workflows, sign makers, and suppliers who request it.

**SVG.** For web use and digital applications. A wordmark SVG is compact, scalable, and ideal for web logos.

**PNG with transparent background.** Multiple sizes — small for web use, large for high-resolution applications. The transparent background ensures the wordmark sits cleanly on any background colour.

**Reversed version.** The wordmark in white or a light colour for use on dark backgrounds. Every wordmark needs this.

**Mono version.** Single-colour black, for embossed business cards, laser engraving, rubber stamps, and any context where colour isn't available.

One critical note: the text in a professional wordmark must be converted to outlines (or paths) before delivery. Live text in a logo file is not a logo file — it's a document that will render differently on any machine that doesn't have the same font.

[What files a designer should deliver](/blog/what-files-should-a-designer-deliver) covers the complete file set in detail.

## Famous Wordmark Logos: What Makes Them Work

Looking at successful wordmarks reveals what the format does when it's done well.

**Google** — the Googleplex lettering uses a custom geometric sans-serif with a multi-colour treatment that makes it immediately recognisable. The slight irregularity in the letterforms (the lowercase 'g' and 'l' have distinctive quirks) makes it feel human despite its geometric foundation.

**Coca-Cola** — a script wordmark with letterforms that have been refined for over a century. The specific connections between letters, the signature flourish on the 'C', and the specific weight of the strokes are entirely custom. No font in the world produces Coca-Cola's wordmark.

**FedEx** — a sans-serif wordmark that famously contains a hidden arrow in the negative space between the 'E' and 'x'. The logo communicates speed and precision while appearing simple. This is what sophisticated wordmark design looks like — intentionality that most viewers never consciously notice.

**Visa** — two words, two colours, geometric sans-serif. Simple enough to work at any size, from a credit card microprint to a stadium banner. The simplicity is the design.
