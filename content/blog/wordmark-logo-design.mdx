Some of the most recognisable logos in the world have no symbol at all.

Google. Coca-Cola. FedEx. Visa. Netflix.

What makes them recognisable isn't an icon. It's the name itself, set in a typeface so deliberately chosen that it becomes inseparable from the brand.

That's a wordmark. And when it's done well, it's one of the most powerful logo formats you can choose.

---

## What a Wordmark Logo Actually Is

A wordmark is a logo built entirely from typography.

It's the full business name — nothing else. No icon beside it. No symbol underneath it. No graphic element framing it.

Just the name, set in a typeface that's been chosen or customised to carry the brand's character on its own.

The power of a wordmark is direct. Every time someone sees it, they see the name. There's no intermediary symbol to associate and then translate back to the business.

The name is the logo. The logo is the name.

This directness is what makes wordmarks so effective for name recognition. Every single impression builds direct awareness of the business name — not of a symbol that then needs connecting.

---

## Wordmark vs. Lettermark vs. Combination Mark

These logo types are often confused with each other. Here's how wordmarks differ from the two most similar alternatives.

**Wordmark vs. lettermark.** A wordmark spells out the full business name. A [lettermark](/blog/lettermark-logo-design) uses only the initials — IBM, CNN, HBO.

The choice usually comes down to name length. A two-word business name is usually a wordmark candidate. A five-word name is usually a lettermark candidate.

**Wordmark vs. combination mark.** A [combination mark](/blog/combination-mark-logo-design) pairs a symbol with a wordmark — icon and name together.

A pure wordmark is simpler and more direct. But it lacks a standalone icon for contexts where one is needed: app icon, favicon, embroidered badge.

This is a real strategic decision. It depends on the name's length, its distinctiveness, and where the logo will be used most frequently.

## The Wordmark Decision Framework

Before committing to a wordmark, run through these five criteria. A strong yes to all five means a wordmark is likely right. More than two no answers suggests you need a combination mark or symbol.

**Criterion 1: Is the name short enough?** Wordmarks work best at one to three words, under 15–18 characters. Test it: does the name fit legibly on a business card at 10mm height? If the letters become unreadable at that size, a wordmark alone won't work at the sizes a logo actually gets used.

**Criterion 2: Is the name distinctive?** A wordmark builds recognition of the name. If the name is generic or shared with other businesses, the wordmark can't do the work — there's nothing memorable to anchor to. "Smith Consulting" as a wordmark faces a harder path than "Stripe" or "Figma."

**Criterion 3: Does the business need a standalone icon?** Think about your use cases. Do you need a favicon? An app icon? An embroidered badge on merchandise? Profile photos on social media? If yes to any of these, a wordmark-only approach will cause problems — most wordmarks don't compress to icon size without becoming unreadable.

**Criterion 4: Are you building name recognition from scratch?** For a new business, every visual impression is a chance to build name recognition. A wordmark guarantees every impression includes the name. A symbol requires a secondary association to form first.

**Criterion 5: Will the logo live primarily in digital contexts?** Wordmarks are highly legible on websites, email signatures, and documents. They scale cleanly. They work in dark mode. They rarely lose detail the way complex symbols can at small sizes.

## When a Wordmark Is the Wrong Choice

A wordmark isn't always right. There are specific situations where another format serves the brand better.

**When the name is too long.** A five-word business name set in any typeface becomes unreadable at small logo sizes. You need either a lettermark or a combination mark where the icon handles small-size applications.

**When you need a symbol for icon use.** A wordmark has no standalone icon. At 32×32 pixels — favicon size — a wordmark is unreadable. At 180×180 pixels — iPhone app icon size — most wordmarks still don't work. If standalone icon use matters for your business, a wordmark alone won't provide it.

**When the brand operates in a category where symbols carry specific meaning.** In healthcare, the cross. In finance, specific geometric marks. Some industries have visual conventions that a wordmark can't replicate or leverage.

**When you need to work without readable text.** Brands operating in global markets, on merchandise, or in contexts where the name isn't visible benefit from having a recognisable symbol. A wordmark only works when people can read it.

## How Wordmarks Are Designed

A good wordmark is not just the business name typed in a font. That's an important distinction.

At the basic level, typeface selection itself is a significant design decision. Serif or sans-serif. Geometric or humanist. Display weight or regular. Each communicates something specific about the brand's character. The typeface needs to match what the brand is trying to say.

At the professional level, wordmarks are customised well beyond the base typeface.

Specific letters are redrawn. Spacing between letters is adjusted precisely for optical balance, not just metric defaults. Specific characters — particularly the first letter or letters — are often significantly altered to create a mark that couldn't be replicated by typing the name.

The most recognisable wordmarks have letter-level customisation that makes them unmistakably themselves.

The Google "G" has been refined through many iterations. The Coca-Cola script has specific letterform connections that are entirely custom. The FedEx wordmark has hidden negative space between the "E" and "x."

This is why professional wordmarks require a designer — not just a font selection tool. The typeface is the starting point, not the ending point. [How to choose logo fonts](/blog/how-to-choose-logo-fonts) covers the specific decisions involved. For a wordmark, this decision carries more weight than in any other logo format — because the type is everything.

## Typography Choices That Define Wordmarks

The typeface category a wordmark uses communicates something specific about the brand. This is one of the most important choices in wordmark design.

**Serif wordmarks** communicate heritage, authority, and institutional stability. Law firms, financial institutions, media companies, and heritage brands often use serif wordmarks. The serifs themselves signal tradition and precision. Think of Vogue, Tiffany & Co., or the Financial Times.

**Sans-serif wordmarks** communicate modernity, clarity, and approachability. Tech companies, startups, and brands targeting contemporary audiences typically use geometric or humanist sans-serifs. They feel clean and forward-looking. Google, Airbnb, and Spotify all use geometric sans-serif wordmarks.

**Script and handwritten wordmarks** communicate creativity, warmth, and personality. Luxury brands, food and beverage companies, and personal brands sometimes use script wordmarks. They feel handcrafted and personal. Coca-Cola is the most famous example. The tradeoff: they often lose legibility at very small sizes.

**Custom lettering** means the letterforms are entirely purpose-built — not drawn from any existing typeface. These are the most distinctive wordmarks possible. There's no font anyone can install to replicate the logo. They require the most investment but produce the most ownable result.

## File Requirements for Wordmark Logos

A professional wordmark logo delivery has the same file requirements as any logo — with one critical point specific to wordmarks.

**The text must be outlined.** In a professional wordmark delivery, the type is converted to vector paths (outlines or curves) — not left as live text. If the wordmark is delivered as live type, any machine without that font installed will substitute a different typeface, breaking the logo completely. This is a common mistake in amateur logo delivery.

**Vector source file (AI).** The original editable Illustrator file, with text outlined.

**EPS.** For print production workflows, sign makers, and suppliers who request it.

**SVG.** For web use. A wordmark SVG is compact, scalable, and ideal for web logos at any screen density.

**PNG with transparent background.** Multiple sizes — small for web use, large for high-resolution applications.

**Reversed version.** The wordmark in white or a light colour for use on dark backgrounds. Every wordmark needs this variant.

**Mono version.** Single-colour black, for embossed business cards, laser engraving, rubber stamps, and any context where colour isn't available.

[What files a designer should deliver](/blog/what-files-should-a-designer-deliver) covers the complete file set in detail. For a wordmark specifically, confirm the text is outlined before accepting any file delivery.

## Famous Wordmark Logos: What Makes Them Work

Looking at successful wordmarks reveals what the format does when executed well.

**Google.** The Googleplex lettering uses a custom geometric sans-serif with multi-colour treatment. The slight irregularity in specific letterforms — particularly the lowercase "g" and "l" — makes it feel human despite its geometric foundation. The colour system makes it instantly recognisable even when the letterforms aren't fully readable.

**Coca-Cola.** A script wordmark refined over a century. The connections between specific letters, the signature flourish on the initial "C," and the specific stroke weights are entirely custom. No font produces Coca-Cola's wordmark. The familiarity comes entirely from that custom lettering being seen billions of times over 130 years.

**FedEx.** A sans-serif wordmark that famously contains a hidden arrow in the negative space between the "E" and "x." The logo communicates speed and precision while appearing simple. This is what sophisticated wordmark design looks like — intentionality that most viewers never consciously notice but the subconscious registers.

**Visa.** Two words. Two colours. Geometric sans-serif. Simple enough to work at any size — from credit card microprint to stadium banner. The simplicity is the entire design strategy. At that level of reproduction volume, complexity becomes a liability.

The pattern across all of them: the name is short, distinctive, and treated with enough typographic care that it becomes a mark in its own right.

## Wordmark vs. Combination Mark: How to Decide

This is the most common logo format decision. Here's the practical way to think through it.

Choose a wordmark if: your name is under 15 characters, you primarily work in digital contexts, you're building name recognition from scratch, and you don't need a standalone icon.

Choose a [combination mark](/blog/combination-mark-logo-design) if: your name is longer, you need a standalone icon for social media or app use, you operate in physical spaces where the symbol does work independently, or you plan to expand internationally where the name may not translate.

Choose a lettermark if: your name is four or more words, the abbreviation is already known in your industry, or you want to shift brand recognition from a founder's name to the institution.

The decision is a [brand identity design](/blog/what-is-brand-identity-design) decision, not just a visual one. The right format depends on how and where the brand will actually be used.
