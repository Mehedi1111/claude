What files should a designer deliver? It's a question most clients only think to ask after they've been handed a JPEG and nothing else.

A logo file is not one file. A complete professional logo delivery is a folder of files, each one serving a different purpose. If your designer gave you a single image file, you're missing most of what you paid for.

Here's the complete breakdown.

---

## What Are Vector Files and Why Are They Non-Negotiable?

Vector files are the foundation of any professional logo delivery.

Unlike raster images (JPEG, PNG, TIFF), vectors aren't made of pixels. They're made of mathematical paths — curves, lines, and shapes defined by coordinates. This means they scale to any size without losing quality. Your logo looks identical whether it's on a business card or a 10-metre billboard.

The main vector formats you should receive:

**AI (Adobe Illustrator)** — the primary source file. This is the editable original, the file a future designer would open to make changes, create variations, or prepare your logo for new applications. This is the most important file in the delivery.

**EPS (Encapsulated PostScript)** — a legacy vector format that works in older design software and most print production workflows. Printers, sign makers, and embroidery suppliers often request EPS. Even if you never open it yourself, it'll be requested eventually.

**PDF (Print-Ready)** — vector data embedded in a PDF. Clean, portable, universally readable. Print suppliers, accountants producing your business stationery, and new team members sharing your logo in presentations all benefit from having this.

If your designer gives you any of these formats, you have the vector. If they don't — or if they tell you they don't share source files — [what is a vector file](/blog/what-is-a-vector-file) explains exactly what you're missing and why it matters for every future use of your brand.

## What Raster Files Should Be Included?

Raster files are pixel-based. They're not as flexible as vectors, but they're essential for digital use because most websites, social media platforms, and document software work with raster files natively.

**PNG with transparent background** — your most-used daily file. Transparent background means the logo can sit on any coloured surface without a white box around it. You need this for your website header, email signature, social media profile images, and anywhere the logo appears over a coloured background.

A complete delivery should include PNG at multiple sizes: small (for web/favicon use), standard (for most digital applications), and large (for high-resolution digital use like presentations and social media headers).

**JPEG** — useful for documents, email attachments, and contexts where transparency isn't needed and file size matters. JPEG doesn't support transparency, so it's not a substitute for PNG — it's an additional file for different contexts.

**SVG for web** — a vector format that web browsers can read natively. Unlike AI and EPS files, SVG can be embedded directly in a website or used in web apps. If you're ever asked to upload your logo to a website builder or app and it asks for SVG, this is the file you need. It's also infinitely scalable at zero additional file size — important for performance.

## What Colour Variants Should You Receive?

A single colour version isn't a complete brand delivery.

**Full colour version** — your primary logo in its intended colours, for use on white or light backgrounds.

**Reversed/white version** — your logo in white (or light colour), for use on dark backgrounds, coloured banners, photography overlays, or dark merchandise. Without this, you'll find yourself trying to use your full-colour logo on dark surfaces where it disappears.

**Single colour / mono version** — a version that works in pure black. This is used for embossed business cards, rubber stamps, embroidery, laser engraving, fax documents (still relevant in some industries), and any context where colour printing isn't available. Some designers provide both a black version and a white (knockout) version.

**Greyscale version** — for contexts where colour is unavailable but a full black logo is too stark. Useful for photocopied documents, certain print contexts, and some legal or government filings.

Each of these variants should exist in both vector (AI/EPS) and raster (PNG) formats. That's a lot of files — and it should be.

## What About Brand Guidelines?

For a logo-only project, brand guidelines may be a separate service. For a brand identity project, they should be included.

Brand guidelines (also called a style guide or brand book) document how your brand is used consistently. The minimum a useful brand guidelines document includes:

**Logo usage rules** — clear zones (the minimum space around the logo), minimum size, what to do and what never to do.

**Colour palette** — exact values in HEX (web), RGB (digital), CMYK (print), and Pantone (for brand-critical print like packaging). If your designer only gives you a HEX code, you have a web colour — not a complete colour palette. [What brand guidelines should include](/blog/brand-guidelines-what-they-include) covers everything a proper style guide documents.

**Typography** — your brand fonts, where they're used, and in what hierarchy.

**Do's and don'ts** — specific visual examples of correct and incorrect logo usage. These protect your brand consistency when the logo is used by people outside the original project.

## What File Formats Are Used for Specific Applications?

Different uses require different files. Here's the practical map:

| Use Case | File You Need |
|---|---|
| Website logo | SVG or PNG (transparent) |
| Email signature | PNG (transparent), small size |
| Social media profile | PNG, correct platform dimensions |
| Business cards (print) | AI, EPS, or print-ready PDF |
| Embroidery / merch | AI or EPS (supplier will specify) |
| Signage / large format | AI, EPS, or vector PDF |
| Presentations | PNG or SVG |
| App icon | PNG at multiple sizes |
| Favicon | ICO or SVG |

If you're going into any of these use cases and realise you don't have the right file, that's a sign your original delivery was incomplete. If your logo was delivered in JPEG only, it can't be properly used for professional print applications — the quality won't hold.

The fix for an incomplete delivery is either to go back to your original designer and request the missing files, or to have a new designer rebuild your logo as a proper vector. The second option is more common than you'd think — [AI logo cleanup and vectorization](/services/ai-logo-vectorization) is specifically designed for businesses that have a logo image but not the proper source files.

## Who Owns the Files?

This is the question most people forget to ask upfront.

There are two models:

**Full ownership transfer** — you receive the source files and own the logo completely. You can use it however you like, modify it, license it, or hand it to any future designer. This is what most reputable logo and brand agencies provide.

**License only** — the designer retains the source files and licenses you the right to use the logo. This is more common in cheap logo services and some agencies. It means any future change requires going back to the original designer, who may no longer exist or may charge significant fees.

Ask before you start: "Will I receive the original source files, and will I own them completely?" Get the answer in writing. This protects you from being locked into a relationship with a designer who turns out to be unreachable in three years when you need your logo adapted for a new application.
