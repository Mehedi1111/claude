Most B2B companies dramatically underestimate what a brand overhaul involves. They budget for a logo and discover they need a positioning framework. They plan for three weeks and find themselves in month four. They think they need one decision-maker and realise they need to align six.

The project runs better when everyone knows what's coming. Here is a complete timeline — phases, deliverables, and what both sides need to do at each stage.

---

## Before the Timeline Starts: The Brief and Scope Alignment

Every brand overhaul begins with a brief. Not a casual email — a structured document that captures the commercial situation (what's changed, what's not working, what success looks like), the project scope, the budget, the timeline expectation, and who makes decisions on the client side. See the [brand brief guide](/blog/brand-brief-guide) for the full brief structure.

Before any timeline can be meaningful, the scope must be confirmed. A brand strategy engagement has a different timeline from a brand strategy plus full visual identity plus website redesign. Mixing these without defining the scope produces a timeline that's meaningless from day one.

---

## Phase 1: Discovery (Weeks 1–3)

### What happens

The first three weeks are about gathering evidence, not producing output. A brand overhaul built on assumptions about the market, the audience, and the competitive landscape will produce work that looks right but performs poorly.

Discovery activities typically include:
- Stakeholder interviews (founders, senior leaders, key client-facing team members)
- Customer or client interviews (3–6 interviews with real buyers or existing clients)
- Competitive brand audit (analysing how 5–8 competitors position visually and verbally)
- Existing brand audit (what's working, what isn't, what equity is worth retaining)
- Market category analysis (how buyers describe their problem and who they compare)

### Client responsibilities in Phase 1

- Make stakeholders available for 45–60 minute interviews
- Provide introductions to clients willing to be interviewed
- Supply existing brand materials: current guidelines (if any), logo files, presentation templates
- Confirm the list of competitors to include in the audit

### Deliverable at the end of Phase 1

A discovery report: documented findings from interviews and audits, with a preliminary view of the strategic territory available to the brand.

---

## Phase 2: Brand Strategy (Weeks 4–6)

### What happens

The discovery findings inform the strategic framework. This is where the agency proposes the core brand architecture: positioning, target audience definition, brand values, personality, and messaging framework (the core messages for different audiences and contexts).

Brand strategy is the hardest phase to schedule because it requires the deepest thinking — and the most difficult client conversations. Challenging existing positioning assumptions is uncomfortable. Clients who discover their intended positioning is indistinguishable from three competitors must decide whether to differentiate more aggressively or accept a competitive disadvantage.

Strategy presentations typically go through two rounds: an initial presentation of the strategic territory (often showing two or three directional options), and a refined presentation after feedback that moves toward a single approved strategy.

### Client responsibilities in Phase 2

- Attend the strategy presentation with all key decision-makers present (not sequentially — this doubles the round count)
- Give structured, specific feedback: what's right, what's wrong, what's unclear
- Approve the strategy before visual identity work begins — changes to strategy after visual work has started reset the visual work

### Deliverable at the end of Phase 2

Approved brand strategy document: positioning statement, audience definition, values, personality, messaging framework.

---

## Phase 3: Visual Identity — Concept Development (Weeks 7–9)

### What happens

With an approved strategy, the design phase begins. Concept development typically produces two or three distinct visual directions — different interpretations of the approved strategy expressed through logo, colour, typography, and supporting elements.

A visual direction is not a finished logo — it's a developed concept with enough detail to see how the direction would perform in context. At this stage, each direction is usually presented with:
- The primary logo mark
- A colour palette
- A typographic system
- One or two mock-ups showing the direction in context (letterhead, presentation slide)

### Client responsibilities in Phase 3

- Review concepts with the approved brand strategy as the evaluation framework (not personal preference)
- Select one direction to develop — not a hybrid of two (hybrids rarely work as well as either original direction)
- Provide specific, actionable feedback: what works, what doesn't, and why

### Deliverable at the end of Phase 3

Approved visual direction for development.

---

## Phase 4: Visual Identity — Development and Refinement (Weeks 10–12)

### What happens

The chosen direction is developed into a complete visual identity system. This includes:

- Logo system: primary lockup, secondary lockup (stacked/horizontal variants), symbol only, wordmark only, reversed versions, single-colour versions
- Full colour system: primary palette, secondary palette, Pantone/CMYK/RGB/HEX specifications
- Typography system: primary and secondary typefaces, hierarchy specifications, licensed font recommendations
- Supporting graphic elements: patterns, textures, icon style, illustration style (if applicable)
- Usage guidelines for each element

This phase typically includes one to two refinement rounds based on client feedback before final approval.

### Client responsibilities in Phase 4

- Review the full system holistically, not element by element
- Confirm all mark variants work for your specific use cases (embroidery, vehicle graphics, app icon — these are the time to flag, not after delivery)
- Provide final, consolidated approval from all decision-makers

### Deliverable at the end of Phase 4

Approved visual identity system, ready for brand guidelines and asset production.

---

## Phase 5: Brand Guidelines and Asset Production (Weeks 13–15)

### What happens

The final phase converts the approved identity into two outputs:

**Brand guidelines document:** A comprehensive reference for anyone creating brand materials — how to use the logo, the colour system, typography, and supporting elements. Typically 30–60 pages depending on the system's complexity. The full breakdown of what goes into it is in the [brand guidelines guide](/blog/brand-guidelines-what-they-include).

**Core asset production:** The actual files your team and partners will use. This includes logo files in all required formats (AI, EPS, SVG, PNG, PDF — across all variants and colour versions), and any core templates included in the scope (presentation deck, letterhead, email signature, social media profile images).

### Client responsibilities in Phase 5

- Provide a list of file formats and sizes needed for specific applications (website, print supplier, embroiderer, signage company)
- Confirm the template list from the original scope
- Arrange the debrief session (a working session where the agency walks through the guidelines and explains usage decisions)

### Deliverable at the end of Phase 5

Complete brand guidelines document, full logo file package, approved templates.

---

## Phase 6: Launch (Week 16)

### What happens

Launch is not an agency deliverable — it's a client operation. But a good agency will support it with:
- A launch communication framework: what to say, when, to which audiences
- Implementation guidance: how to roll out the new identity across digital and physical touchpoints in priority order
- A list of common vendor requirements (what your signage company, printer, and web developer will need)

### The priority order for launch

Not everything can launch simultaneously. The practical priority sequence:
1. Website (highest visibility, most controllable)
2. Email signatures (immediately visible to all contacts)
3. Social media profiles (quick to update, high visibility)
4. Business stationery (ordered as stock depletes, or immediately if client-facing)
5. Physical signage (highest cost to change, often last)

## What Actually Delays Brand Overhaul Projects

**Slow feedback cycles.** A 48-hour turnaround commitment that becomes two weeks across eight rounds adds three months to a 12-week project. Every agency agreement should specify turnaround expectations — and clients should take them seriously.

**Absent decision-makers at presentation.** Presenting to a delegate who needs to "run it by" someone else and then book a follow-up creates a parallel timeline of uncertainty. All key decision-makers must attend every formal presentation.

**Scope creep after phase approval.** Adding a website redesign after the visual identity is approved, or requesting a new service line's collateral after the scope was set, extends the project. These additions should be treated as separate engagements with their own timelines.

**Perfectionism beyond the point of return.** A brand overhaul produces a starting point, not a permanent monument. The visual identity will evolve. Spending three extra months refining the logo corner radius delays the more valuable work of actually using the brand.

---

## The Questions to Ask Your Agency Before Starting

If you need context on what agencies typically offer, read [what a branding agency actually does](/blog/what-does-a-branding-agency-actually-do) before this conversation.

- What does your project phase structure look like?
- How many revision rounds are included per phase?
- What happens if feedback is delayed — do you pause the project or continue?
- Who is our primary point of contact, and who does the actual work?
- What do you need from us to keep the project on schedule?

The answers reveal how structured and experienced the agency is — and what the working relationship will actually feel like.
