Case studies are the highest-converting content on most professional service business websites. They do what no other format can: they prove, with specific evidence and named outcomes, that you solved a real problem for a real client and produced a measurable result. A visitor reading a well-constructed case study is not being told you are capable — they are watching you be capable in a documented, verifiable way. Most case studies fail not because the work was poor, but because the case study describes process (what the agency or consultant did) rather than demonstrating results (what the client achieved). This guide covers how to structure, write, and design case studies that convert.

---

## What Makes a Case Study Persuasive?

A case study persuades when it does three things:

1. **Describes a recognisable problem.** The prospective client reading the case study must see their own situation in the client's situation. "A professional services firm in London with a 5-year-old website that was not ranking in Google and generating fewer than 2 enquiries per month" is a recognisable, specific problem. "A business that wanted a better online presence" is not.

2. **Shows specific work and decision-making.** Not just the finished deliverable — but enough of the process to demonstrate the thinking behind it. Why was this approach chosen over alternatives? What specific problem did each design or strategic decision solve?

3. **Quantifies the outcome.** "The redesigned website launched in April 2026. By July, organic search enquiries had increased from 2 per month to 14 per month. The site now ranks on the first page of Google for [target keyword]." This is the sentence that converts readers into enquirers.

## What Is the Standard Structure for a Case Study?

**Overview / summary panel:** At the top of the case study, a quick-reference summary: client name (or sector if confidential), industry, services provided, project duration, and one key outcome statistic. This lets skim-readers immediately assess relevance before committing to the full read.

**The client:** Who the client is, what their business does, and why they matter in their market. 1–2 sentences. The goal is to give context, not to sell the client.

**The challenge:** The specific problem, constraint, or opportunity that brought the client to the project. This is the most important section for resonance — a prospective client who sees their own challenge here is immediately engaged.

**The approach:** The thinking and key decisions that led to the solution. Not a step-by-step process description — a narrative of why specific choices were made. "We chose Next.js over Webflow because the client's content team needed a CMS capable of supporting 200+ blog posts with complex filtering, which Webflow's CMS could not accommodate at that volume."

**The work:** Visual documentation of the output — design mockups, screenshots, photography, video demos. This is where the quality of the work is demonstrated visually.

**The outcome:** Quantified results. Traffic numbers, conversion rates, revenue impact, time saved, awards won, press coverage generated. The more specific the better. Avoid vague outcomes ("the client was very happy with the result") in favour of measurable ones.

**Client testimonial:** A direct quote from the client, ideally referencing the specific outcome. Adjacent to the outcome section, this provides third-party validation of the results.

**Related projects or next steps:** Link to similar case studies and include a CTA to start a conversation about a similar project.

## What Photography and Visual Documentation Standards Apply?

**For web design and development projects:**
- Professional device mockups showing the finished website on laptop, desktop, and mobile simultaneously
- Screenshots of specific pages that demonstrate the design quality (homepage, key service page, portfolio section)
- Before/after comparison if a redesign — showing the original versus the new design is often the most compelling visual in a web design case study
- Short screen recording or GIF showing animations, interactions, or CMS functionality where relevant

**For branding and identity projects:**
- The brand system applied across multiple touchpoints: logo, business card, stationery, signage, website, social media
- Mockups in realistic contexts — business cards in hand, stationery on a desk, signage on a building
- Typography and colour palette displayed clearly

**For strategy and consulting projects:**
- Frameworks, diagrams, or process visualisations created during the engagement (appropriately anonymised where necessary)
- Data charts showing before/after metrics where relevant
- Document and report covers if deliverables included written strategy work

## How Should Case Studies Be Organised on the Website?

**Dedicated case studies or work page:** A filterable gallery of case study cards — each showing the client name, industry, primary service, and a hero image. Filterable by industry (Technology, Healthcare, Retail) or by service (Web Design, Brand Identity, Consulting).

**Case study length:** The right length is determined by the decision complexity for the prospective client. High-value B2B purchases ($20,000+): 600–1,200 words. Complex multi-service engagements: longer, with detailed sections for each phase. Straightforward project wins: 300–500 words with strong visual documentation.

**Frequency:** Publishing a case study for every significant project — even if brief — builds a body of evidence over time. A business with 20 detailed case studies across multiple sectors is far more persuasive than one with 3 long-form case studies. Aim for quantity and quality: regular, well-documented case studies.

**SEO value:** Case studies with specific client names, industry sectors, and outcome metrics contribute to long-tail SEO. "Web design for London law firm case study" or "Brand identity design for healthcare startup" are low-volume but high-intent searches that case study pages can rank for. See [website seo guide](/blog/website-seo-guide) for the SEO framework.

## How Do You Get Clients to Approve Case Studies?

**Build case study documentation into your project process.** The best time to request approval for a case study is at project completion — when client satisfaction is highest and the outcome is fresh. Not six months later when the relationship has moved on.

**Use a simple approval process.** Share a draft case study document (Google Doc or PDF), ask for factual corrections and outcome confirmation, and request any testimonial quote. Most clients who were happy with the project will approve a well-written, accurate case study quickly.

**Offer confidentiality for sensitive information.** If a client is uncomfortable disclosing specific revenue or conversion metrics, offer to describe the outcome as a percentage ("organic traffic increased by over 300%") or a range ("enquiries increased from under 5 per month to over 20 per month") rather than an absolute number.

**Offer anonymisation as a fallback.** If a client will not approve a named case study, an anonymised version — "a professional services firm in London" or "a Series A SaaS startup in Sydney" — is significantly better than no case study at all.
