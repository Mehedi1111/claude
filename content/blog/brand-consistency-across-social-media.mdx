Open your LinkedIn profile. Then open your Instagram. Then your Facebook page.

Look at them side by side. Same logo? Same colours? Same bio language?

For most service businesses, the answer is no. The logo is a different crop. The bio describes the business differently on each platform. The colours in the cover images don't match. None of this was intentional. It just happened — one platform updated at a time, over months or years, with no one checking whether everything still cohered.

This is brand inconsistency. And it's costing you more than you think.

---

## Why Brand Inconsistency Costs You Clients

Here's what happens when a prospect searches your business name.

They find you on LinkedIn. They click through to your website. Then they check your Instagram. Then they might look at your Facebook page.

If each one looks slightly different — different logo, different colours, different bio that emphasises different things — they can't form a clear picture of who you are. They start to wonder if you're organised. If your service will be consistent. If you actually care about the details.

They don't usually articulate this. They just feel uncertain. And uncertain prospects don't enquire.

The research on this is consistent: people need multiple touchpoints before they buy from a service business. Every touchpoint where your brand looks or sounds inconsistent adds friction to that journey.

This doesn't require a big team or expensive tools to fix. It requires a system.

---

## The 6-Point Consistency Framework

There are six things that must match across every platform. Get these right and everything else falls into place.

### 1. Your Logo or Profile Photo

This one seems obvious. It usually isn't.

Profile photos display as circles on most platforms. That means your horizontal wordmark — the one that looks great on your website header — is unreadable at profile photo size. You need either a symbol-only version of your logo, a monogram, or a professional headshot if you're a personal brand.

Pick one approach. Use it everywhere. Not a different crop on each platform. Not the old logo on one and the new one on another. The same file, correctly sized for each platform.

Keep a folder with your profile photo at the correct dimensions for each platform. When you need to update, you update the folder and every platform uses the same source file.

### 2. Your Brand Name Spelling

You'd be surprised how often this is inconsistent.

"Evoke Studio" and "EvokeStudio" and "EVOKE" and "evoke.studio" are four different names. If you use different versions on different platforms, your brand feels fragmented.

Pick the exact spelling your business uses. Use it exactly on every platform. Including capitalisation, spacing, and punctuation.

### 3. Your Bio Language and Core Message

Your bio doesn't need to be word-for-word identical across platforms. Character limits won't allow it. But the core message — who you serve, what you do, why someone should care — should be the same substance in every version.

Write one master bio, 150–200 words, that says everything important. Then compress it for each platform without changing the substance.

When you update your positioning — when you niche down, enter a new market, or change your service focus — update every platform in the same sitting. Not one this week and another next month.

### 4. Your Colour Palette

Any branded graphics you post — cover images, banners, post templates — should use your actual brand colours.

Save your hex codes somewhere accessible. Not in your head. Not in an old Canva project you have to hunt for. In a shared document or brand folder that's open whenever you're creating anything.

Your LinkedIn banner, your Instagram highlights covers, your Facebook page header — they should all feel like they come from the same brand. Same colour family. Same visual personality.

### 5. Your Cover Images

LinkedIn, Facebook, X, and YouTube all have header image spaces. Most businesses either leave them blank or use something outdated.

Your cover images don't need to be identical. But they should feel related. Same colours. Same general visual approach. Same level of professionalism.

When you update one, update all of them. This takes 30 minutes. It's worth it.

### 6. Your Link

Every platform with a bio link should point to the same destination. Usually your website homepage or a specific landing page you're actively using.

Don't send different platforms to different places unless you have a deliberate, active reason. An inconsistent destination makes your digital presence feel disconnected.

## The 20-Minute Brand Audit

Before you fix anything, you need to know what's broken. This audit takes 20 minutes.

Open every platform where your business has a presence. For each one, screenshot the profile page. Put all screenshots side by side — either on a second monitor or in a shared Google Slides document.

Then work through this checklist:

**Logo check:**
- Same image on every platform?
- Is it the current logo, or an old version?
- Does it crop correctly as a circle?
- Is it high resolution — not blurry when you zoom in?

**Name check:**
- Same exact spelling on every platform?
- Same capitalisation?

**Bio check:**
- Does the core message — who you serve and what you do — say the same thing on every platform?
- Do any bios reference old services you no longer offer?
- Is the tone consistent — formal on one, casual on another?

**Colour check:**
- Do your cover images and any branded graphics use the same colours?
- Would someone looking at all your profiles think they came from the same brand?

**Link check:**
- Does every platform link to where you want people to go?
- Are any links broken or outdated?

Note every gap. You'll fix them in order of how visible the platform is to your target clients.

## Platform-by-Platform: What to Fix

Different platforms have different requirements. Here's the specific guidance for each.

### LinkedIn

LinkedIn is the highest-priority platform for almost every B2B service business.

**Profile photo:** 400 × 400px minimum, displays as circle. Use your headshot or logo mark — not the full wordmark.

**Background banner:** 1584 × 396px. This is prime real estate. Most people leave it as the default grey gradient. Your banner should be branded — your colours, your name, a short positioning statement, or imagery that reflects your work.

**Headline:** Your headline shows up in search results, in connection requests, and under your name everywhere on LinkedIn. Don't just put your job title. Put your positioning. "Brand identity design for professional service businesses" tells a prospect what you do immediately. "Founder at Evoke Studio" tells them nothing without clicking through.

**About section:** LinkedIn gives you 2,600 characters. Use the first two sentences to state your positioning clearly. Visitors don't have to click "see more" to get to the key message.

**Company page:** If you have a company page, it needs its own logo (300 × 300px for the profile image), a branded cover image (1128 × 191px), and an About section that matches your personal page positioning. They should feel like the same brand.

### Instagram

Instagram is visual-first. Your grid, your profile photo, and your story highlights all create an immediate visual impression.

**Profile photo:** 320 × 320px, displays as circle. Logo mark or headshot.

**Bio:** 150 characters. You have one line to state what you do and who it's for. Don't waste it on emojis or vague language. "Brand identity + web design for service businesses → contact link below" is better than "✨Creative Studio | Design | Branding | Web✨".

**Story highlights:** Branded covers in your colour palette. Unmade highlights — the default grey circles — look unfinished. You don't need many. Five well-organised, branded highlights with clear titles look better than fifteen chaotic ones.

**Link in bio:** Make sure it goes to the right place. Check it. A broken link in an Instagram bio is invisible to you and noticeable to everyone else.

### Facebook

Facebook has lower organic reach than LinkedIn or Instagram for most service businesses, but prospects still check it.

**Page profile photo:** 170 × 170px, displays as circle on desktop. Same logo or headshot as everywhere else.

**Cover photo:** 820 × 312px. Branded, consistent with your other platforms.

**About section:** Short description that matches your other bios. Don't write a different version here.

**Services section:** If your Facebook page has a services section, make sure it reflects your current offering — not services you dropped two years ago.

### X (Twitter)

**Profile photo:** 400 × 400px.

**Header:** 1500 × 500px. Branded.

**Bio:** 160 characters. Your compressed positioning statement.

**Location:** Set this to your city or country. Profiles without location information feel anonymous.

### YouTube

If you have a YouTube channel — even a dormant one — it shows up when people search your business name.

**Channel art:** 2560 × 1440px (displays differently on different devices — keep key content in the centre 1546 × 423px safe zone).

**Profile photo:** Same as everywhere else.

**Channel description:** Should match your bio language and positioning.

## The Brand Reference Document

The audit fixes the past. A reference document prevents future drift.

This doesn't need to be a full brand guidelines document — that's a bigger project. It's a simple, accessible one-page document that anyone updating your social profiles can use.

It should contain:

**Your brand name:** The exact spelling, capitalisation, and punctuation to use everywhere.

**Your logo files:** Links to the current profile photo file, ready to use. Not "in the old Google Drive folder from 2023." A live link to the current file at the right size.

**Your hex colour codes:** Your primary and secondary brand colours, in hex. Anyone creating a graphic for your brand can reference these instantly.

**Your bio copy:** Long version (for LinkedIn and Facebook About) and short version (for Instagram and X). When you update your positioning, you update this document — and then update all platforms from this single source.

**Your link:** Where every profile currently points.

Share this document with everyone who might ever update your social profiles. Pin it in your team's Slack or Notion. Send it to any freelancer who creates content for you before they start work.

The moment this document exists, your brand consistency becomes a system rather than a memory test.

## When to Do a Full Brand Check

Set a quarterly reminder. Every three months, spend 20 minutes going through the audit checklist.

The biggest risk is change. Every time you update your logo, refresh your website, change your positioning, or adjust your service offering — every one of those moments is a risk of inconsistency if you update some platforms and forget others.

Build the habit: when you change anything about your brand on any platform, change it everywhere in the same session. Don't save the other platforms for later. Later becomes never.

For a more formal version of this system — one that covers your visual identity across every touchpoint, not just social media — [brand standards documentation](/blog/what-are-brand-standards) is the complete reference. Most businesses need this the moment anyone other than the founder is making brand decisions independently.

## What Good Brand Consistency Actually Looks Like

Here's the test.

Ask someone who doesn't know your business well to spend five minutes looking at your website, your LinkedIn, your Instagram, and one other profile. Then ask them: does this feel like one brand?

If they say yes without hesitation, you've got it right.

If they pause, or say something like "mostly," or point to differences they noticed — that's your audit starting point.

Consistent brands feel inevitable. When someone finds you on LinkedIn and then clicks to your website, it should feel like a natural continuation. Same colours. Same voice. Same level of professionalism. The same business.

That's not a big-agency luxury. A small service business can achieve it with a one-page reference document, a quarterly 20-minute audit, and the discipline to update everything at once when something changes.

It's one of the highest-ROI brand improvements you can make. And it costs nothing except attention.
