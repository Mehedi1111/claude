Two consulting firms quoted the same client for a brand strategy engagement. The quality of their thinking, as far as the client could assess at the proposal stage, was equivalent.

One firm's proposal was formatted in Calibri, the default Microsoft font. Clean, inoffensive, invisible.

The other was set in Sharp Grotesk, with deliberate typographic hierarchy, generous leading, and clear section structure.

The client chose the second firm. When asked why, she couldn't articulate it precisely. "They just looked more serious," she said.

Typography did that. Not the thinking — the visual treatment of the thinking.

## Why Typography Is a Trust Signal, Not a Decoration

Most businesses think of typography as a design preference. Experienced brand designers understand it as a trust signal.

A typeface carries associations. These associations are not arbitrary — they've been built through decades of use in specific contexts. Seeing a particular typeface in a particular context, the viewer's pattern recognition system fires before conscious thought: *this looks like the things I trust*.

The associations aren't rigid. But they're real enough to affect how a document, a website, or a presentation is perceived before a single word is absorbed. Typography sets the register — the level of authority, care, and intentionality — that everything else is interpreted against.

A legal brief set in a clean, authoritative typeface with proper hierarchy reads as more credible than the same text set in a generic default font. Not because the words changed. Because the visual treatment of the words changed how seriously they're taken.

This is the ROI of typography. Not in any single transaction, but in the accumulated impression it creates across every touchpoint where the brand makes an impression.

## The Four Ways Typography Affects Brand Performance

### 1. Perceived authority

The typeface you use signals what category of business you're operating in and at what level within that category. A management consultancy using a premium typeface associated with established professional services — a well-spaced serif, or a sophisticated geometric sans — looks like it operates at the level where the fees are high and the work is serious.

The same consultancy using the default Microsoft Office typeface — Calibri, Arial, Times New Roman — looks like it hasn't thought about this. Which implies it hasn't thought carefully about other things either.

Authority perception isn't the only factor in winning a client. But it's a factor in every early impression — and early impressions determine whether the relationship develops or not.

### 2. Readability and cognitive load

Good typographic design — appropriate type size, generous line spacing, clear hierarchy between headings and body, adequate contrast — makes content easier to read.

Easier to read means less mental effort. Less mental effort means the reader spends more cognitive resource on the content itself and less on decoding the format.

The practical business impact: a proposal that's easy to read gets read more thoroughly than one that requires effort. A website that's well-set keeps visitors longer than one with tight leading and insufficient contrast. A brand that communicates clearly at the typographic level is a brand that communicates more effectively everywhere.

See the [brand guidelines guide](/blog/brand-guidelines-what-they-include) for how typography should be documented for consistent use.

### 3. Brand consistency

A defined typographic system — specific typefaces, specific weights, a hierarchy specification that everyone uses — is one of the most powerful consistency tools in a brand system.

When every document, presentation, and digital asset is set in the same typefaces with the same hierarchy logic, the brand becomes recognisable across contexts. The recognisability is subconscious — the viewer doesn't consciously identify the typography — but the accumulated impression of coherence builds brand recognition faster than any single element could.

When the typographic system is undefined or ignored, the opposite happens. Different team members use different fonts based on what's installed on their machine. Presentations don't match the website. Proposals don't match the letterhead. The brand looks like it was made by different people who don't talk to each other — because at the typographic level, it was.

### 4. Fee level signalling

This is the most commercially direct impact of typography. Premium brands — in professional services, luxury goods, or high-ticket B2B services — use premium typography. This is not accidental.

The relationship between typographic quality and perceived fee level is consistent enough to be a deliberate strategy. If your business charges at the top of its category and its typography reads as generic or default, there's a gap between the claim (premium) and the evidence (not premium). Prospects who notice this gap discount accordingly.

The inverse is also true: investing in typographic quality signals, quietly but clearly, that this is a business that sweats the details. Which is exactly the signal you want to send to high-value clients deciding which firm to trust with serious work.

## Common Typography Mistakes That Hurt Brand Performance

**Using system fonts by default.** Calibri, Arial, and Times New Roman are perfectly functional typefaces. They're also the typographic equivalent of a navy suit from a supermarket — fine for the context, but not communicating anything about care or investment. Default system fonts signal that no typographic decision was made.

**Using too many typefaces.** A common mistake in self-built brands is using different typefaces for every element. The heading is one font, the body is another, the caption is a third, the pull quote is a fourth. Visual chaos where there should be visual system. A professional typographic system uses one or two typeface families with different weights to create hierarchy.

**Poor hierarchy.** Typography creates hierarchy: heading, subheading, body, caption. When every element is the same size and weight, the reader has to work to understand the structure of the content. Effective hierarchy makes structure immediately visible.

**Wrong weight and spacing choices.** A light typeface weight on a white background at small sizes fails basic readability. A bold typeface for long body copy is fatiguing. Too-tight line spacing (leading) makes text feel dense and uninviting. These details matter disproportionately in the documents and presentations where high-stakes decisions get made.

**Ignoring dark mode and print considerations.** A typographic system designed only for screen in light mode will fail on dark backgrounds and on print. A professional system includes specifications for all contexts.

## How to Evaluate Your Current Typography

Collect ten recent pieces of brand output — your website, a recent proposal, your email signature, your presentation template, a social media post, your business card. Lay them next to each other (screenshots work for digital).

Ask:
- Do these all use the same typefaces, or are there different fonts across documents?
- Is the hierarchy — heading size, body size, caption size — consistent?
- Does the type treatment look intentional, or like the defaults were left in place?
- Does the typographic quality match the fee level you're charging?

If the answers reveal inconsistency or mismatch, you have a typographic system problem. The solution is a defined typographic system in your [brand guidelines](/blog/brand-guidelines-what-they-include), followed by templates that enforce it.

---

## What a Professional Typographic System Looks Like

A complete typographic system for a professional brand includes:

- **Primary typeface:** The main brand font, used for headings and display elements
- **Secondary typeface:** A complementary typeface for body text (sometimes the same family in a different weight)
- **Type scale:** Defined sizes for H1, H2, H3, body, caption, and any other levels needed
- **Weight specifications:** Which weights of each typeface are used at which level
- **Colour specifications:** Primary type colour, secondary type colour, reverse (on dark) specifications
- **Spacing guidelines:** Line height for body copy, heading spacing, paragraph spacing
- **Web-safe fallbacks:** What renders if the brand font fails to load in a browser

This system lives in the brand guidelines document and is embedded in every template. When everyone uses the same templates built on the same typographic system, consistency happens without effort.

## The Investment Calculation

A professional typeface licence for a three-person studio or small consultancy: £200–£600 per year. For a larger team: £1,000–£3,000 per year depending on the licence terms.

The alternative: looking like you haven't thought about your typography for years, in every impression your brand makes on every prospect and client.

One client where the typographic quality pushed the proposal over the line — one meeting where the visual treatment of your thinking made you look more serious than the competitor — returns the investment many times over.

Typography is not a luxury. It's an infrastructure cost in a professional services business where every impression counts.
