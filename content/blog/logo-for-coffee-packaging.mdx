A specialty coffee roaster spent three months developing their first blend. The sourcing story, the roast profile, the cupping notes — all exceptional. The beans were genuinely good. When the bags arrived from the packaging printer, the logo — a detailed hand-lettered script over a vintage illustration — was barely visible. The fine lines had nearly disappeared in the print, and the illustration details looked muddy against the kraft bag texture.

The designer hadn't specified any of the technical requirements for the substrate. The logo wasn't wrong in itself — but it had never been tested against how ink behaves on kraft paper.

Coffee packaging is one of the most design-competitive retail categories. When someone walks into a specialty grocery or café, the shelf is full of thoughtfully designed bags from roasters who understand that the packaging is part of the product experience. Here's how to make your logo and brand identity work at this level.

## The Packaging Surfaces a Cafe Brand Uses

Understanding which surfaces need your logo helps prioritise file preparation:

**Coffee bags (retail):** The primary brand expression. Stand-up pouches, valve bags, or flat-bottom bags in kraft, matte, or glossy finishes. Logo appears prominently on the front panel.

**To-go cups:** Paper cups in 8oz, 12oz, 16oz sizes. Full-colour printing or sleeve printing. Logo appears on the cup body or sleeve.

**Cup sleeves:** A separate sleeve (holder) applied to the cup. Some cafes print the full brand only on the sleeve and use plain cups underneath, which significantly reduces printing costs.

**Coffee boxes / carry trays:** For multiple drinks. Logo on the top or side panel.

**Labels (for retail bags sealed without printed packaging):** A label applied to a plain bag, printed separately. This is a more flexible and lower-cost approach for smaller roasters.

**Takeaway bags / paper bags:** For food orders. Logo printed on flat or gusseted bags.

**Cup lids:** Some brands print the lid. Less common due to plastic surface printing complexity, but used in premium contexts.

**Business cards and loyalty cards:** Always part of the cafe brand ecosystem, following the same visual language.

## Logo Adaptation for Coffee Packaging

### The bag panel challenge

Coffee bag printing uses flexographic printing for most production runs, or digital printing for small runs. Both have technical requirements that differ from standard paper printing:

**Flexographic print requirements for bags:**
- Vector file with spot colour separation (one layer per colour)
- Minimum stroke width: 0.5pt (lines thinner than this either disappear or spread into adjacent ink)
- Minimum text size: 7pt for sans-serif, 9pt for serif
- No photographic or continuous-tone gradients unless the printer confirms digital capability
- Pantone colour references required

**Digital bag printing:**
- Accepts high-res raster or vector
- Supports photographic gradients and full colour
- No minimum run requirement (can print single bags)
- More expensive per unit, but more flexible

**What this means for detailed logos:** A complex illustration or fine-lined hand lettering may need simplification for flexographic bag printing. The level of detail achievable depends on:
- The quality of the printer and their equipment
- The substrate (how much the material spreads ink)
- Whether your printer has experience with illustration-heavy designs

If your logo has intricate detail — common in coffee branding, which loves hand-drawn aesthetics — test with a small run before committing to large production.

### Substrate-specific challenges

**Kraft paper (natural brown):** The brown background changes how colours read. White ink is often needed to create a background for other colours, or the design has to be planned around the brown substrate. White ink on kraft is expensive — it requires an additional plate and pass. Budget accordingly.

**Matte white bags:** Closest to standard paper printing. Colours read accurately. Most predictable for brand colour reproduction.

**Glossy bags:** Vivid colour reproduction, high-impact shelf appearance. More expensive. Logo colours appear more saturated than on matte.

**Clear bags (window bags):** Part of the bag is transparent, showing the product. Logo placement must account for the window area — placement on the opaque portion only.

**Compostable/recycled materials:** These speciality substrates can affect ink adhesion and colour reproduction differently. Always request a press proof on the actual material.

### Cup printing specifics

Paper cups are printed before they're formed into cup shape. The flat sheet is printed, then wrapped and sealed. This means the artwork is designed on a flat template that accounts for the cone shape — the design needs to be "unrolled" to account for the cylindrical distortion.

**Key specs:**
- Artwork template provided by the cup manufacturer (essential — don't guess the dimensions)
- Most cup templates include "wrap" areas and a seam allowance
- Digital printing: 300 DPI minimum at actual substrate dimensions
- Offset cup printing: vector with colour separation

**Common mistake:** Designing the cup artwork in a flat rectangle and sending it. Cup artwork has a tapered width — the base of the cup is narrower than the top. A flat rectangular design will look distorted when wrapped around the cup.

Always get the die-line template from your cup supplier before designing.

### Sleeve design

Cup sleeves are flat rectangles that wrap around the lower portion of the cup. The design must work on a rectangular flat surface.

**Standard sleeve dimensions:** approximately 4.5" × 3" when flat, but varies by supplier. Get the template.

**Design approach:** The sleeve is often the most visible logo surface at eye level when someone holds a drink. It's prime brand real estate. Large logo mark, clean layout, no clutter.

## What Makes Coffee Packaging Logo Design Different

Coffee packaging succeeds when the design communicates origin story, craft, and character simultaneously. The logo in this context isn't just a brand identifier — it's part of a visual narrative.

Some approaches that work well:

**Illustration-integrated logos:** A logo mark that incorporates imagery (coffee plant, mountain, map element, abstract pattern) relevant to the brand's origin or story. Works best when the illustration is clean enough to reproduce at packaging constraints.

**Bold typographic logos:** Clean, impactful wordmarks in distinctive fonts. Strong on shelf, reproduces cleanly in any printing method.

**Minimal geometric marks:** Abstract symbol with a clean wordmark. High versatility across all packaging surfaces. Works at any size.

What tends not to work:

**Overloaded badge logos:** A circle with five lines of text, an illustration, a tagline, and a founding date. At 2 inches wide on a bag, none of it reads. Badge logos need to be designed with minimum print size in mind.

**Ultra-light weight typefaces:** Beautiful on screen, invisible on a kraft bag at 8pt.

**High-fidelity photography elements:** Won't reproduce on flexographic printing and looks cheap when approximated in CMYK.

### Preparing the logo system for packaging

A complete cafe brand logo system for packaging should include:

1. **Primary logo in vector** — with all text outlined, CMYK colour mode, clean paths
2. **Reversed/white version** — for dark backgrounds and kraft substrate applications
3. **Single-colour version** — for one-ink print runs (most cost-effective for labels and bags)
4. **Simplified/icon-only version** — for small applications (cup lid, loyalty card stamp)
5. **Colour specification** — Pantone references plus CMYK and HEX for each brand colour
