A streetwear brand founder spent three months developing his first collection. The garments were well made, the fabrics excellent, the silhouettes strong. He'd designed the logo himself in Photoshop: bold text with a custom graphic he'd drawn by hand.

When he approached manufacturers for woven labels, the first thing they asked for was a vector file. He didn't have one. The logo existed as a 400px PNG.

When he submitted it to the print shop for chest prints, they rebuilt it freehand from the PNG — and the proportions came out wrong. The embroidery sample didn't match the print sample. The hang tag version looked different from both.

Three months of product development and the brand had four inconsistent versions of its logo across a single first collection.

This is the production reality that every clothing brand founder encounters, usually at the worst possible time.

## How Clothing Brand Logos Are Different

A clothing brand logo has more physical production contexts than almost any other business type. Consider every place a clothing brand logo appears:

- **Woven label** inside the garment (woven thread, minimum detail)
- **Screen print** on chest, back, or sleeve
- **Embroidery** on chest, collar, or cap
- **Rubber patch** on back waistband or wrist (common in streetwear)
- **Heat transfer label** (PVC or TPU, replacing traditional sewn labels in many brands)
- **Hang tag** (printed on card, attached with string before sale)
- **Tissue paper** or packaging (screen print, sticker, or stamp)
- **Sticker** (attached to packaging or distributed)
- **Social media** (profile image, content)
- **Website**

Each context has different file requirements, different minimum sizes, different colour limitations, and different technical constraints. A logo designed without awareness of these production realities will fail in several of them.

## Design Principles for Clothing Brand Logos

**Extreme scalability.** A clothing label is often read at a few centimetres in a dimly lit changing room. The logo must remain legible at that scale. Every element — letterforms, any symbol, the arrangement — must hold at very small sizes.

**Simplicity in service of production.** Every additional design element is another thing that can go wrong in production. Fine details fill in during embroidery. Thin strokes disappear on woven labels. Gradients can't be reproduced in most garment applications. The most production-friendly clothing logos are built on clean, bold, simple forms.

**Weight and stroke consideration.** The minimum stroke width for most production methods is 2mm. Fine hairline serifs, thin script letterforms, and delicate details will not survive embroidery, woven label production, or small-format screen printing. Test any proposed logo at actual production size before committing to it.

**Symbol and wordmark relationship.** Clothing brands often use a symbol independently (embossed on buttons, used as a chest mark, applied to accessories) while the full combination mark appears on labels and packaging. Design both elements with their independent use in mind.

## Typeface Choices for Clothing Logos

Typeface choice communicates where in the market the brand positions itself — and it communicates this before the garments are seen.

**Bold sans-serif:** The dominant choice in streetwear, contemporary sports, and direct-to-consumer casualwear. Legible, bold, production-friendly. Can range from classic (Helvetica-adjacent) to custom geometric depending on brand personality.

**Serif with character:** Established luxury brands often use serifs — but serifs that hold up at small print sizes require careful selection. Old Style serifs with well-proportioned thin and thick strokes (rather than hairline serifs that collapse at embroidery size) are safer for apparel production.

**Custom letterforms:** The most distinctive option and the most expensive. Custom letterforms are inherently ownable and immediately recognisable as belonging to a specific brand. Supreme, Palace, Off-White, A-Cold-Wall — their wordmarks are recognisable specifically because they're custom. For brands building long-term value, custom type is worth the investment.

**Script or handwritten:** Works well for heritage brands, brands with a strong personal story, and certain lifestyle categories (surf, outdoor, artisanal). Technical challenge: scripts have thin strokes and complex paths that fail in embroidery and small woven labels. If using script, a production-safe simplified version is always necessary.

## Production Methods and What Each Needs

### Woven labels

Woven labels are sewn into garment seams or the collar. The logo is woven in thread, which means:
- Minimum thread width: approximately 1–2mm
- Text minimum: 3mm character height
- Colour count: typically 2–4 colours (more colours increase cost significantly)
- No fine detail, no thin strokes, no gradients
- Provide the woven label manufacturer with a clean vector EPS with simple, defined shapes

### Embroidery

Chest logos, collar embroidery, cap embroidery. Requirements are similar to woven labels. The difference: embroidery is digitised separately from the logo design — a professional digitiser converts the vector into a stitch file. Quality of digitisation matters enormously.

See the complete embroidery requirements in the [logo embroidery guide](/blog/ai-logo-embroidery-requirements).

### Screen printing

The primary method for large garment prints (chest graphics, back prints). Requires colour separation — one screen per colour — and clean vector paths. The logo needs each colour on a separate layer in the vector file.

For file preparation and quantity economics, see the [screen printing vs DTG guide](/blog/screen-printing-vs-dtg-logo).

### Heat transfer labels (PVC/TPU)

Increasingly common in contemporary fashion brands replacing sewn-in labels. A custom shape, often following the logo outline. Requires vector files and awareness that the label will be applied at heat press temperatures — certain fine details can warp.

### Hang tags

Printed on heavy card stock, typically 350–400gsm, with a hole punched for a string. The logo appears alongside brand information, size, care instructions, or marketing copy. Minimum logo size on a hang tag: approximately 20mm wide. Provide the printer with vector files and specified CMYK values.

### Rubber patches

A moulded silicone or rubber patch, often placed at the back waistband. Very common in premium denim, outdoor brands, and streetwear. The logo must be extremely simple — maximum 2 colours, bold forms, no thin details. The moulding process cannot reproduce fine detail.

## Building the Apparel Brand Beyond the Logo

Strong clothing brand identities extend the logo into a coherent visual system:

**Care labels and size labels:** The interior brand experience. These are often overlooked but contribute to the perception of a professional, established brand.

**Packaging:** Mailer boxes, tissue paper, stickers, poly bags with the brand mark. Packaging is increasingly a brand moment — customers share unboxing experiences.

**Lookbook and campaign photography:** The visual aesthetic of brand photography (art direction, model casting, locations) becomes as much a part of the brand as the logo. The logo should be designed in relationship to the photography direction, not in isolation.

**Social media grid:** Instagram is still the primary discovery channel for most clothing brands. The logo, used consistently as a profile mark, should be legible at 110px in a circle.
