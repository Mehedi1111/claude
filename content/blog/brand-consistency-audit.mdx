We once audited a client's brand before starting a refresh project. They were a five-year-old consulting firm, about fifteen people, well-regarded in their space. They'd had their logo professionally designed at launch.

In twenty minutes of audit work, we found:

- Three different versions of their logo in active use (the original, a recreation someone had done in Word, and a low-res JPEG that had clearly been screenshotted from their website)
- Four different hex values being used for their "brand blue" across different pages of their website
- Their email signature used a font that wasn't in their brand palette
- The footer of their proposal template still said 2021
- Their LinkedIn banner hadn't been updated since they moved offices in 2023 and still showed their old address
- Two team members had profile photos with wildly different backgrounds from everyone else

None of this was intentional. It was just the accumulated drift of five years of growth with no one actively managing the visual identity. Each individual problem was minor. Together, they made the brand feel like a company that didn't have its act together — which was not true and not the signal they wanted to send.

This audit is the process we used. You can do it yourself in about 20 minutes.

## Step 1: Collect Every Place Your Brand Appears (5 minutes)

Before you can audit anything, you need to know everywhere your brand lives. Write a list. Here's a starting point:

**Digital touchpoints:**
- Website (every page — header, footer, about, services, blog, contact)
- Email signatures (check every person on your team, not just your own)
- Social media profiles (LinkedIn, Instagram, Facebook, Twitter/X, YouTube — profile image, banner, bio link)
- Google Business Profile
- Email marketing templates
- Any directories or listings (Clutch, Yelp, industry directories, Google Maps)
- Digital proposals and pitch decks

**Print and document touchpoints:**
- Business cards
- Letterhead
- Invoice templates
- Proposals and quote templates
- Any printed marketing materials (brochures, flyers, postcards)
- Packaging or labels (if applicable)

**Environmental touchpoints:**
- Office signage
- Vehicle graphics
- Branded merchandise or uniforms

Don't skip this step. The audit only catches what you look at. The inconsistencies that cause the most damage are often the ones you've stopped noticing — the invoice template you've used for three years without looking at closely.

## Step 2: Check Logo Consistency (5 minutes)

Open or access each item on your list. For each one, check:

**Is it the current logo?** Compare against your master logo file. If you don't have a master file or you're not sure which version is current, that's a finding in itself.

**Is it the correct version for this context?** The horizontal version in the website header. The stacked or icon version on social media profiles. Not the horizontal logo crammed into a square. See [horizontal vs. stacked logo](/blog/logo-horizontal-vs-stacked) for the rules on this.

**Is it the correct background version?** Dark background should have the reversed (white) logo. Light background should have the primary (dark) logo. This is one of the most common failures.

**Is the size appropriate?** Not microscopic, not enormous. Proportional to the material.

**Is there adequate clear space?** Is any other element crowding the logo? Text, images, or borders overlapping the logo's required clear space are a brand standards violation.

Rate each touchpoint: correct, needs updating, or missing entirely.

## Step 3: Check Colour Consistency (5 minutes)

This requires a specific tool. Open the colour picker in your browser developer tools (right-click → Inspect on any element in Chrome) and sample the brand colours you see across your website. Write down the exact hex values.

Then open every document and design file you can access and sample the same. Check:

**How many different values are being used for each "brand colour"?**

When I audited that consulting firm's website, I found their brand blue was used as #0056B3 on the homepage hero, #0057B7 on the services page, #0052AF in the footer, and #1A6DB8 in the blog. Same colour visually — different hex values that produce slightly different shades. Someone had been eyeballing it instead of using the documented value.

**Are the right colours being used in the right places?** Some brands have a primary colour and an accent. If the accent is being used as the primary, or vice versa, that's a consistency failure.

**Are any off-brand colours appearing?** Off-brand blues in someone's email signature. A slightly different grey in an older brochure. These are usually innocent — someone picked "close enough" — but they accumulate into visual noise.

Document the correct hex, RGB, and CMYK values for every brand colour. If you don't have these documented, that's the underlying problem — see [brand guidelines explained](/blog/brand-guidelines-what-they-include) for how to fix it.

## Step 4: Check Typography Consistency (3 minutes)

**What fonts are being used in the wild?** Pull up your website, your email signature, your proposals, your social media graphics. Are they all using the same brand fonts?

Common failure patterns:
- Someone creates a slide presentation in PowerPoint, which substitutes a system font when the brand font isn't installed
- An email template was set up using whatever fonts the email platform defaulted to
- Social media graphics were made in Canva by someone who chose a typeface they liked rather than the brand typeface

**Are font sizes and weights consistent?** Headings should be roughly the same weight and size across similar materials. Body text should be a consistent size. This doesn't mean every document needs to be pixel-perfect, but dramatic inconsistency — 12pt body copy in one document and 9pt in another — reads as visual disorganisation.

## Step 5: Spot-Check Photography and Imagery (2 minutes)

This is the softest check but often where the most visible inconsistency lives.

**Do the images across your website look like they belong to the same brand?** Wildly different photography styles — professional product shots alongside phone snapshots alongside generic stock — create a fragmented brand feeling even when the logo and colours are consistent.

**Are team photos consistent?** Different backgrounds, different lighting, different cropping. One team photo in warm tones, another in cold. This is very common and very noticeable.

**Is stock photography consistent in style?** If you use stock images, are they stylistically consistent? Or is it a mix of different libraries, different eras, different aesthetic directions?

## Recording Your Findings

Keep a simple spreadsheet: Touchpoint | Finding | Priority | Owner | Status. This makes the audit actionable rather than just a list of problems.

Prioritise by visibility and impact:
- **Critical:** Active materials that clients or prospects see frequently (website, email signature, proposals, business cards)
- **Medium:** Less frequent but still client-facing (brochures, proposals, event materials)
- **Low:** Internal materials or rarely-used assets

Fix the critical ones first. Work through the rest systematically. The goal is a brand that looks like it was designed by one person, today — not a collection of materials produced by different people over different years with no shared reference.

## What to Do When You Find Problems

**Logo version problems:** Get the correct file from your brand designer or from the most recent source of truth. If there's no clear source of truth, that's the problem to fix — establish which file is the master and put it somewhere the whole team can access it. See [logo file naming system](/blog/logo-file-naming-system) for how to organise this.

**Colour inconsistencies:** Document the correct hex values. Update every digital touchpoint manually. Brief anyone who creates branded materials with the correct colour codes.

**Font inconsistencies:** Ensure the brand fonts are installed on every team member's computer, and added to your email platform, Canva, and any other tools used for branded content.

**Old content:** Update it or take it down. An old address, an old logo, old photographs — these signal a brand that isn't maintained.
