A law firm briefed four agencies. Three showed portfolios of their best work — varied, impressive, industry-agnostic. One showed exclusively professional services work: two law firms, an accounting practice, and a management consultancy.

The fourth agency got the project.

Not because their work was better. Because the partner scrolling through their portfolio thought: *these people understand our world*.

This is the entire logic of industry-specific portfolio curation. Not to show everything you've done — to show the prospect exactly what they need to see.

---

## Why Generic Portfolios Lose to Targeted Ones

A prospect evaluating agencies isn't asking "who does the best design work?" They're asking "who can I trust to understand my industry, my clients, my constraints?"

A restaurant logo in a law firm proposal answers none of those questions. It's visual noise. It might be beautifully designed, but it creates cognitive distance — the prospect has to work to imagine how this applies to them.

Industry-specific curation removes that work. It lets the prospect immediately see themselves in your output. It signals that you've thought about them specifically, not just shown up with a broad selection.

This matters most in professional services, B2B, and high-stakes categories — where clients are buying judgment and expertise alongside execution, and where the visual vocabulary of a well-run brand is very different from consumer categories.

---

## The Curation Framework: Four Questions

Before selecting what to include in an industry-targeted portfolio presentation, answer four questions:

**1. What is the visual register of this industry?**

Every industry has a dominant visual register — the range of colours, typographic weight, image style, and layout density that clients and customers expect. Legal is navy, dark green, serif type, restrained layout. Fintech is bold, geometric, often dark mode, clean sans-serif. Hospitality is warm, textured, photography-led.

Your selected pieces should demonstrate fluency in their register — or show how you deliberately worked within it, then pushed it.

**2. What problems do clients in this industry have that your work solves?**

A fintech company's brand problem is usually trust and differentiation. A law firm's is often looking established without looking old. A consulting firm's is looking authoritative enough to justify their fee level.

Select pieces that solve recognisable versions of these problems. Your case study writing should name the problem explicitly so the prospect can map it to their own situation.

**3. What is the sophistication level of their in-house team?**

A venture-backed startup with no design team needs different evidence than an enterprise with an internal brand team looking for strategic support. Match the complexity of your selected work to the complexity of their situation.

**4. What action do you want them to take after viewing the portfolio?**

If you want an enquiry call, make the portfolio provocative and incomplete — leave obvious questions unanswered that only a conversation can resolve. If you want them to feel confident enough to send a brief immediately, make it thorough and reassuring. The curation serves the goal.

## Building an Industry-Specific Portfolio Section

For a serious targeting strategy, create industry-specific portfolio sections rather than a single chronological archive. This requires more maintenance but pays off significantly in conversion.

**The structure:**

Each industry section should contain:
- Three to five projects, documented as proper case studies (not just images)
- A brief positioning statement at the top: why you work well with this type of client
- Outcome data where available: metrics, client quotes, before/after comparisons
- One piece that demonstrates something unexpected — showing range within the category

**The case study format:**

Each piece should answer: what was the brief, what was the problem underneath the brief, what did you make, why did you make those specific choices, and what happened after.

The "why" is the most important part. Anyone can show an attractive logo. The industry-specific case study shows that you understood *why* that visual approach was right for *that type of business* in *that market context*.

## When You Don't Have Industry Work Yet

The most common objection to industry-specific curation: "I haven't worked with anyone in that industry."

Three approaches:

**1. Speculative work, done properly**

Commission yourself. Choose a real business in the target industry — a local firm, a brand you know — and produce a full brand identity for them as a self-initiated project. Present it as such. "Speculative project — unsolicited redesign concept" is transparent and legitimate. Many agencies have won significant clients on the strength of speculative projects that demonstrated both skill and strategic thinking.

**2. Adjacent industry work with bridging case study writing**

A hospitality brand and a luxury retail brand aren't the same industry, but they share a visual register: warmth, texture, premium positioning. A case study that explicitly draws this connection — "our work in luxury hospitality prepared us for the visual challenges of premium retail" — lets you use work you have to demonstrate readiness for work you want.

**3. Reduced-rate entry project**

Offer a significantly reduced rate to a target-industry client in exchange for full portfolio and case study rights. One good entry piece, documented properly, is worth more than years of hoping to win your first project in a new category.

---

## The Presentation Format Matters

How you present industry-specific work matters as much as the selection.

**Tailored PDF over public portfolio:** For a serious pitch, a customised PDF that opens with the prospect's industry, references their type of challenges, and shows only relevant work is more effective than directing them to a general portfolio website. The extra effort is visible — and valued.

**Lead with the thinking, not the aesthetics:** Industry-specific prospects are often buying expertise, not just execution. Open each case study with the strategic problem, not the visual solution. A fintech company doesn't primarily care that the logo looks clean — they care that you understood why their target audience distrusted competitor brands, and designed to address that.

**Use their language:** Every industry has vocabulary. Use it correctly. "Series A founder," "fee-earner," "AUM," "pipeline conversion" — whichever terms your target industry uses professionally. Using insider vocabulary accurately signals that you've worked in this world, or at least studied it seriously.

---

## Maintaining Multiple Targeted Portfolios

If you're targeting more than one industry — which most studios are — the maintenance overhead is real. A few principles:

**Build modular case studies.** A project for a management consultancy might appear in both your "professional services" section and your "typography-led identity" section. Write the case study once in full, then create abbreviated versions that emphasise different aspects depending on context.

**Update quarterly, not constantly.** Set a calendar reminder every quarter to review each industry section. Add new work, retire anything that no longer represents your current standard, and update outcome data.

**Let your best work in each category be the deciding factor.** The question isn't "how much can I show?" — it's "what is the single best piece that proves I understand this industry?" Build out from there.

---

## The Difference Between Showing Work and Proving Expertise

A portfolio is evidence. Industry-specific curation is about making the evidence as relevant and legible as possible to the specific person making a hiring decision.

Generic portfolios make prospects do the work of imagining how your skills apply to them. Targeted portfolios do that work for them.

The edit is the expertise. Showing everything you've ever made is not the same as showing a prospect exactly what they need to see to trust you with their brand.
