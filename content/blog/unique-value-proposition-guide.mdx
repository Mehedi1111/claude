Most businesses don't have a unique value proposition. They have a description of what they do.

"We provide high-quality design services for businesses of all sizes." That's a description. It answers what. It doesn't answer who, why, or compared to what.

A unique value proposition is different. It's specific enough to attract the right prospect immediately and filter out the wrong one before either of you wastes time.

Here's how to write one that actually does that.

---

## What a UVP Actually Does

A unique value proposition answers three questions simultaneously.

**What do you do?** Not a list of services — the outcome or transformation you deliver. Not "we design logos and websites" — what that results in for the client.

**Who is it for?** Not "businesses" or "entrepreneurs" — the specific type of person or organisation for whom this is most valuable. Specific enough that the right person reads it and thinks "that's me."

**Why you and not someone else?** The specific reason this business is the right choice for this person over the available alternatives. Not "we care about quality" — an actual differentiator.

When all three are answered in one statement, you have a UVP. When any one is missing, you have something that sounds like a UVP but doesn't function as one.

---

## The Difference Between a UVP, a Tagline, and a Mission Statement

These three things are frequently confused. They're different enough that confusing them produces the wrong thing for each purpose.

**Tagline.** A short, memorable phrase for brand recall. Usually poetic, emotional, or clever. "Think Different." "Just Do It." "Because You're Worth It." A tagline communicates the brand's personality and values in a form that sticks in memory. It doesn't need to explain what the business does — that's not its job.

**Mission statement.** A statement of the business's purpose — why it exists, what it's trying to achieve in the world. Usually written for internal alignment and external perception of values. "To provide everyone in the world access to information" is a mission statement. It describes aspiration, not offer.

**Unique Value Proposition.** A statement of specific value for a specific person. External-facing, written for the prospect. Answers what, who, and why in specific terms. Less emotional than a tagline, more specific and offer-focused than a mission statement.

A business can have all three. Most businesses mix them up and end up with none of them working properly.

---

## Strong vs. Weak UVPs: Real Examples

The difference between a UVP that works and one that doesn't is usually specificity.

**Weak:** "We help businesses reach their potential through creative design and strategic thinking."

This says nothing specific. Any design business could say this. "Businesses" doesn't describe anyone in particular. "Reach their potential" isn't an outcome. "Creative design and strategic thinking" is a description of activities.

**Strong:** "Brand identity and web design for B2B professional service firms who are ready to compete with larger agencies."

This says something specific. It names a clear audience (B2B professional service firms). It describes a real situation the audience is in (ready to compete with larger agencies). It specifies the offering (brand identity and web design). A professional service firm in this position reads it and thinks "that's exactly us."

**Weak:** "We build beautiful websites that help your business grow."

Every web design business says some version of this. It's not wrong, but it doesn't differentiate.

**Strong:** "We build marketing-led websites for consulting firms — designed to convert senior decision-makers, not just look professional."

This names the audience (consulting firms), the benefit (convert senior decision-makers), and the implicit differentiator (most websites only look professional without being built to convert). A consulting firm reading this knows immediately whether this is for them.

**Weak:** "Quality accounting services you can trust."

Quality and trust are claimed by every accounting firm. This communicates nothing distinctive.

**Strong:** "Tax strategy and accounting for tech founders — we help you structure your business before the complexities of fundraising, secondaries, and exits."

This names the audience (tech founders), the specific moment of value (before fundraising complexities), and implies the expertise that makes this firm the right choice (they know what fundraising, secondaries, and exits mean for your tax situation). A tech founder preparing to raise reads this and knows this firm understands their world.

## Why "We Help X Achieve Y" Is Usually Not Enough

You'll find a lot of advice that suggests a UVP follow the formula: "We help [target audience] achieve [outcome] through [method]."

This formula is better than nothing. It forces specificity on at least two dimensions. But it often produces UVPs that are technically correct and still don't work.

"We help small business owners build more profitable companies through strategic financial planning." True. Specific audience. Specific outcome. Specific method.

Still not unique. Fifty other accounting and advisory firms could say the same thing.

A UVP that works adds one more element: the specific reason why this business is the right choice over the alternatives. Not a claim that anyone can make (quality, care, experience) — but something genuinely specific to how this business operates, who it serves, or what it does differently.

This is the hardest part to write. It requires honest clarity about what you actually do differently — not what you aspire to do differently.

---

## The Three-Part UVP Framework

This is the structure we use at Evoke Studio when developing positioning for clients. It works for almost any service business.

**Part 1: The audience + their specific situation.**
Not just who they are, but where they are. "B2B consulting firms who've outgrown freelance designers but aren't ready for a large agency." "Solicitors practices moving from their founding partner's name to an institutional brand." "Early-stage SaaS companies building their first real brand before Series A."

**Part 2: The specific outcome you deliver.**
Not services — results. "A brand identity that makes them look like the category leader they're becoming." "A visual identity that holds up in enterprise pitch rooms." "A website that converts senior buyers, not just looks professional."

**Part 3: The specific differentiator.**
Why this business and not another. "We're the only studio that starts with brand strategy before any design begins." "We only work with professional services firms — which means we understand the client acquisition challenges your firm actually faces." "Every project is led by the same senior designer from briefing to delivery — no handoffs to juniors."

Assemble these three elements into two or three sentences, and you have a working UVP.

## Testing Your UVP: The Three Questions

Before committing to a UVP, test it against three questions.

**Could a competitor say exactly the same thing?** If yes, the UVP isn't unique enough. The differentiator section needs to be more specific. "High quality service" and "experienced team" can be said by everyone. Find the thing that's genuinely specific to your business.

**Does the right prospect immediately recognise themselves?** Ask someone who matches your target profile to read the UVP and say whether it describes them. If they pause or say "I think so, maybe," the audience definition is too vague.

**Does it make a promise the business can actually keep?** A UVP that claims something the business can't consistently deliver damages trust the moment a client discovers the gap. The UVP should be the honest expression of what you're already doing well, not aspirational language about what you'd like to do.

## Where the UVP Connects to the Wider Brand

A UVP isn't a standalone statement — it's the surface expression of your brand positioning.

The [brand positioning statement](/blog/brand-positioning-statement-guide) is the internal strategic document that defines exactly where your brand sits in the market. The UVP is the external-facing communication of that position — the form in which it appears on your website and in your marketing.

The [brand messaging framework](/blog/brand-messaging-framework) takes the UVP and extends it across all the channels where your brand communicates — website copy, LinkedIn, proposals, email. The UVP is the seed; the messaging framework is everything grown from it.

Getting the UVP right first makes everything downstream easier. Website copy becomes cleaner because the positioning is clear. LinkedIn profiles become sharper because the message is specific. Proposals become more convincing because the value being offered is precisely articulated.

The reverse is also true: if the UVP is vague, everything built on it will be vague. A website that doesn't convert, social profiles that don't attract, proposals that don't land — these are often symptoms of a UVP problem, not a design problem.

---

## Common Mistakes in Writing a UVP

**Writing for everyone.** The instinct to appeal broadly produces UVPs that resonate with no one. Narrowing the audience feels like leaving revenue on the table. In practice, a narrow UVP produces better-quality enquiries, higher close rates, and stronger referrals. The right prospects feel specifically addressed. The wrong ones self-select out — which saves everyone time.

**Leading with services rather than outcomes.** "We offer brand identity, web design, and brand strategy" is a menu, not a proposition. What does the client get? What changes for them? Lead with outcome.

**Using claim language that anyone can use.** "Trusted," "experienced," "quality-focused," "passionate," "results-driven." These words add length without adding meaning. A UVP that consists primarily of these words is not a UVP — it's a collection of unsubstantiated adjectives.

**Writing for the business, not the prospect.** A UVP is written for the person reading it, not for the business saying it. Every word should be evaluated from the prospect's perspective: does this tell me something I care about? Does this help me decide whether this is the right choice?

**Never testing it.** A UVP written in isolation and never tested against real prospects or clients is a hypothesis, not a positioning. Show it to people who match your target profile. If they don't immediately understand it and see themselves in it — rewrite it.
