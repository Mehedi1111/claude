A senior partner at a management consultancy had 22 years of experience. She'd advised Fortune 500 companies on market entry strategy across Southeast Asia. Her client outcomes were exceptional by any measure.

Her LinkedIn profile had 340 connections and hadn't been updated since 2019.

When a referral prospect searched her name before a meeting, they found nothing useful. Her credibility existed entirely in the heads of people who already knew her — invisible to everyone outside that network.

This is the experienced professional's personal brand problem. Not that you lack authority. That your authority isn't findable.

---

## Why Experienced Professionals Often Have the Weakest Online Presence

There's a pattern. Early-career professionals build online presence out of necessity — they need to be found, they need to prove themselves. People who built their careers on relationship and referral networks often never needed to.

For twenty years, referrals came in. Clients introduced them to other clients. The phone rang because someone trusted told someone else to call.

Then the referral network plateaus. Or they want to reach a new type of client outside the existing network. Or they want to position for a different type of work. And suddenly the relationship-only approach has a ceiling.

The personal brand problem isn't about becoming an influencer. It's about making existing expertise findable by people who don't already know you.

---

## Step 1: Choose a Specific Positioning (Not a Career Summary)

The first instinct of most experienced professionals is to document everything they've done. The LinkedIn About section becomes a résumé. The website becomes a career timeline.

This is the wrong approach.

A personal brand is not a career archive — it's a positioning statement about what you do now, for whom, and why you're the right choice. Your 22 years of experience is the proof behind the claim, not the claim itself.

Before anything else, answer three questions:

**Who do you most want to work with?** The specific type of client — by industry, by company stage, by problem type — where you do your best work and get your best outcomes.

**What specific problem do you solve for them?** Not your service description — the actual business problem. "I help professional services firms win more enterprise contracts by fixing the gap between their fee level and how they present externally" is a problem description. "Brand strategy and consulting" is a service description.

**Why do you win when you win?** Not your credentials — the actual reason clients choose you over alternatives with similar credentials. This is usually an insight about your approach, your perspective, or a specific methodology you've developed.

The intersection of these three is your positioning. Lead with it everywhere.

## Step 2: Fix LinkedIn Before Anything Else

LinkedIn is where professional reputations get researched. Before a prospect takes a meeting, before a referral reaches out, before a conference organiser considers you as a speaker — they've checked LinkedIn.

The profile elements that matter most for experienced professionals:

**The headline (the line under your name):** Most people use their job title. This is a missed opportunity. The headline appears in search results, in connection notifications, and in every context where someone encounters your name on LinkedIn. Use it as a positioning statement: "Helping professional services firms win enterprise clients through brand strategy" beats "Managing Partner, [Firm Name]".

**The About section (the summary):** Write in first person. Open with the specific problem you solve for the specific type of client you want. Don't open with your career history. End with a clear invitation to connect or reach out.

**The Featured section:** Pin your best three pieces of evidence — a case study, a published article, a speaking recording, a particularly substantive LinkedIn post. This is the first thing an interested visitor looks at after the summary.

**Activity:** LinkedIn's algorithm surfaces you to more people when you're active. You don't need to post daily — two or three substantive posts per week is enough to maintain visibility with your existing network and grow it gradually.

## Step 3: Your Website as the Owned Asset

Social platforms change their algorithms, reduce organic reach, and occasionally disappear. Your website is yours.

For an experienced professional, a personal website doesn't need to be large — it needs to be right. Five pages well done:

**Home:** Your positioning statement, who you work with, what you help them achieve. Plus one piece of immediate evidence — a quote from a recognisable client, a specific outcome, a notable credential.

**About:** The story of how you got to where you are, with emphasis on the experiences that directly built the expertise you're positioning on. Not a résumé — a narrative that creates context for why you specifically are the right choice.

**Work or Expertise:** Case studies or outcome descriptions for your most relevant and impressive work. Three to five is enough.

**Writing or Thinking:** A place to publish your perspective. This doesn't need to be frequent — but it needs to exist. A prospective client who reads two or three pieces of your published thinking and finds it sharp and specific will contact you before they contact someone with equivalent credentials but no visible thinking.

**Contact:** Clear, simple, low-friction. A form, an email address, a link to book a call. Not a wall of text about how to get in touch.

---

## Step 4: One Content Channel, Done Consistently

Content is the mechanism by which your expertise becomes findable by people who don't know you. But the wrong approach — trying to be everywhere, posting inconsistently, writing generic content to fill space — is worse than no content at all.

Choose one channel. LinkedIn articles, a newsletter, a podcast, a YouTube series — whichever you'll actually do consistently. The format that works for you is the right format.

What to write about: your perspective on problems your target clients face. Not general industry news — your specific point of view. What do you see differently from the conventional wisdom? What advice do you give clients that surprises them? Where do you disagree with the mainstream view in your field?

Point-of-view content — content that takes a specific position and argues for it — builds more credibility faster than comprehensive informational content. Anyone can write "here are ten things to consider when rebranding." Only you can write "the biggest mistake professional services firms make when rebranding is treating the visual identity as the rebrand — here's why and what they should do instead."

Avoid common [personal branding mistakes](/blog/personal-branding-mistakes-to-avoid) by publishing your thinking rather than your credentials.

## Step 5: A Visual Identity That Matches Your Authority

This is the most common gap for experienced professionals: the visual identity doesn't match the authority.

A 20-year consultant positioning at the executive level needs a visual identity — headshot quality, website design, PDF and presentation templates — that signals that level of authority. A headshot taken in bad lighting with a cluttered background, a website built on a free template with the platform's branding in the footer, a proposal template formatted in default Word styles — these create the same visual credibility gap as a luxury hotel with a worn lobby carpet.

The visual identity for a personal brand doesn't need to be elaborate. It needs three things to be right:

**A professional headshot.** Book a photographer for two hours. Not a friend with a good camera — a professional photographer who shoots people in professional contexts. The headshot appears everywhere: LinkedIn, website, speaker profiles, email signature. It's doing constant impression work.

**A consistent visual style.** Choose two fonts, two or three colours, and a photography approach, and use them consistently across every touchpoint. You don't need a complex brand system. You need enough consistency that everything looks like it came from the same person.

**A website that matches the fee level.** If you charge £500 a day and your website looks like it cost £200 to build, you're asking prospects to discount the contradiction. The website doesn't need to be award-winning — it needs to look like it was made by someone who invests in quality.

See our guide on [personal brand visual identity](/blog/personal-brand-visual-identity) for the specific elements.

## The Patience Problem

Personal brand building for experienced professionals works — but not instantly.

LinkedIn growth for a professional with a niche, senior positioning typically looks like: three months of almost no visible growth, then a slow acceleration, then occasional posts that reach far beyond the existing network and bring in a wave of new connections and enquiries.

The three-month plateau is where most experienced professionals stop. They conclude it's not working. It's working — it's just working on the timeline of reputation-building, not the timeline of paid advertising.

The experienced professional's advantage is that when the content reaches the right person, it converts. Twenty years of real expertise in a specific area produces content that junior-career personal brands cannot replicate. The audience is smaller, the conversion is higher.

Stick with it for six months before evaluating whether it's working.
