Red flags when hiring a logo designer are easy to see in hindsight. Before you've paid, they can look a lot like enthusiasm, speed, and a great deal.

This guide is about spotting them before you hand over money — not after.

---

## They Start Designing Without Asking About Your Business

This is the most telling red flag of all.

A logo is a visual representation of a business. It needs to communicate something specific to a specific audience. To do that, the designer needs to understand the business, the audience, the competitors, and the feeling the logo should create.

If a designer quotes you, takes your money, and starts sending you concepts without a single question about what your business actually does — you're getting decoration, not design. They're making aesthetic choices in a vacuum because they have no information to make strategic ones.

Good designers ask. They ask about your audience before they pick a style. They ask about your competitors before they choose a visual direction. They ask about your use cases before they settle on a mark type. If none of that happened, the logo you receive is a guess.

---

## Their Portfolio All Looks the Same

A designer whose entire portfolio follows one current trend — geometric minimalism, hand-lettered warmth, maximalist retro — is telling you something.

Either they only know how to work in that style, or they're designing for what looks good in a portfolio rather than what's right for each client. Both are problems.

A strong design portfolio shows range — different industries, different audiences, different visual solutions for different strategic problems. The work should feel like it came from an understanding of each business, not from a personal aesthetic applied universally.

Trendy work has a shelf life. [What makes a logo timeless](/blog/what-makes-a-logo-timeless) is worth reading before any logo project — it clarifies the difference between a logo designed to look good now and one designed to serve the brand for the next decade.

---

## They Can't Tell You What the Process Is

Ask any designer: "Can you walk me through your process?"

If the answer is vague — "we'll have a call, do some research, and send you concepts" — that's not a process. That's a description of events without any thinking about why each one happens.

A real design process has structure because each stage depends on the one before it. Strategy before concepts. Concepts before refinement. Refinement before finalisation. Each stage has a specific purpose and output.

A designer who can't explain their process clearly hasn't thought about it deliberately. And if they haven't thought about how they design, they almost certainly haven't thought about why.

---

## They Promise Unlimited Revisions

This one sounds like a benefit. It isn't.

Unlimited revisions sounds generous. In practice, it often signals one of three things: the designer is planning to charge extra for anything beyond a minimal first concept; their process has no structure so they're covering for it with open-ended revision language; or they expect the work to be generic enough that the revision conversation never gets started.

A well-structured revision process has limits — typically 2–3 rounds — because the limit creates accountability. It means the designer presents strong concepts the first time rather than rushing to show something and banking on the client finding their way through unlimited rounds.

Clear, limited revisions are a sign of a designer who's confident in their process. Unlimited revisions are often the opposite.

---

## They Don't Ask About Your Competitors

Your logo exists in a market. It will be seen alongside competitors. It needs to feel distinct from them — to occupy visual territory they don't, or to deliberately contrast with the category conventions.

A designer who doesn't ask about your competitors designs without knowing what territory is taken. They might accidentally produce something that looks similar to your main competitor. They might follow category conventions when breaking them would serve you better.

Ask early: "Will you do any competitor research before designing?" A designer who says yes — and who can tell you what they'll do with that research — understands that logo design is a strategic act, not just an aesthetic one.

---

## Their Portfolio Is All Mockups, No Real Work

Mockups are presentation tools — lifestyle photos of a coffee cup or a tote bag with a logo applied. They make concepts look finished and real-world.

But a portfolio that's entirely mockups with no evidence of real clients using the work in the real world should raise a question: are these real projects, or concepts that were never used?

Ask: who is the client for this project? Can I see how the logo is being used by the business today? If the answer is "this was a personal project" or "the client hasn't launched yet" or the designer deflects, you're looking at concept work, not proven work.

---

## They Can't Explain Why They Made Design Choices

After presenting concepts, a designer should be able to articulate why each one looks the way it does.

"I chose a rounded mark because your brand is approachable and professional, and the circular form references the community focus in your positioning." That's a designer who made deliberate choices.

"I just thought this one looked good for your industry." That's a designer who guessed.

Ask the question explicitly: "Can you walk me through why you made these specific choices?" The answer tells you whether the work is strategic or decorative. Decorative work looks fine until your business context changes — and then it no longer fits.

---

## The Price Has No Scope

A quote without a scope is a blank cheque.

"Logo design — $1,800" is not a quote. It's a number. What does $1,800 buy? How many concepts? How many revision rounds? What files are included? Is brand strategy part of it? Are guidelines included?

A proper quote lists deliverables. If you receive a price and have to ask what's included, the scope is probably being made up as the project progresses — which is how projects end with unexpected additional charges.

Ask for a written scope before you pay anything. If the designer can't or won't produce one, walk away.

---

## They Retain the Source Files

This is a dealbreaker for most clients, and many don't know to ask until they need the files and find out they don't have them.

Source files are the original editable files — typically Adobe Illustrator AI files. Some designers retain these and only license you the exported logo. This means you can use the logo but you can't modify it, and any future change requires going back to the original designer.

A reputable designer transfers full file ownership including source files as a standard part of delivery. If a designer mentions "license" or "usage rights" rather than "ownership transfer," get clarity on what you actually own before signing anything. [What files a designer should deliver](/blog/what-files-should-a-designer-deliver) covers exactly what you should be handed at the end of any logo project.

---

## They Disappear After Sending the First Concept

Communication pattern during the project predicts communication after it.

A designer who takes two weeks to respond to a brief question, who doesn't acknowledge your feedback within a day or two, or who sends a concept with no follow-up call or message to discuss it — this pattern doesn't improve after delivery.

If you have questions about file usage, need a small adjustment, or discover an issue six months later, you want a designer who's accessible. Ask how they prefer to communicate and what their typical response time is before you start.

---

## They Push Back on Every Piece of Feedback

There's a healthy version of a designer defending their choices — explaining the reasoning behind a decision when a client wants to change something that would actually weaken the work. That's professionalism.

Then there's a designer who treats every piece of feedback as an attack, argues with you over preferences, or makes you feel bad for having an opinion. That's not professionalism. That's a collaboration problem that will make the rest of the project unpleasant.

Feedback is part of the process. A designer who can't hear it gracefully — who can defend their choices without dismissing yours — is going to be exhausting to work with.
