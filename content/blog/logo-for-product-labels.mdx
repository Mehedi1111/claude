A skincare brand ordered their first run of 1,000 product labels. The designer had matched the brand colours on screen — a specific warm beige and a deep burgundy — and the labels looked gorgeous in the PDF proof.

The printed labels arrived two weeks later. The beige was noticeably yellower than the screen version. The burgundy was darker and more purple. The label still looked professional, but it didn't match the product photography on the website. On a shelf next to the website, the brand looked inconsistent.

The issue was RGB-to-CMYK conversion. The designer worked in RGB, the printer converted to CMYK, and the colours shifted. A problem entirely preventable by understanding how print colour works before submitting files.

Product label printing has specific requirements beyond standard print — regulatory text demands, material and finish considerations, and in some industries, legal compliance requirements. Here's everything you need to know before sending your logo to a label printer.

## Label Printing Methods and What They Mean for Your Logo

The printing method your label vendor uses affects what file specifications you need to meet and what your logo will look like at final output.

### Digital label printing

Most short-run label orders (under 5,000 units) use digital printing. A high-resolution inkjet or laser printer applies CMYK inks directly to the label stock.

**Logo requirements:**
- CMYK or RGB (the printer's RIP software handles conversion, but CMYK from your end is safer)
- 300 DPI minimum at print size
- Vector preferred for logos with sharp edges and flat colours

**Strengths:** Fast, affordable for small runs, unlimited colours, excellent for gradients and photographic elements.
**Weaknesses:** Pantone spot colour accuracy is approximate — the printer blends CMYK to simulate Pantone, and the match is never perfect.

### Flexographic printing

Used for large production runs (typically 10,000+ units), especially for food, beverage, and pharmaceutical products. Plates are made for each colour, similar to screen printing but for continuous-feed material.

**Logo requirements:**
- Spot colour separation — each colour on its own layer, Pantone references required
- Vector file mandatory — no raster elements
- Minimum stroke weight: 0.5pt (thinner strokes fill in or disappear)
- Minimum text size: 6pt for serif fonts, 5pt for sans-serif (check with your printer)

**Strengths:** Extremely accurate Pantone colour reproduction, cost-effective at scale, fast.
**Weaknesses:** High setup costs (plates are expensive), not economical for short runs, gradients are difficult.

### Screen printing

Used for specialty label applications — labels on bottles, certain rigid packaging, premium cosmetics.

**Logo requirements:**
- Colour separation required
- Vector mandatory
- Similar constraints to flexographic printing

Know which method your label printer uses before designing. This determines whether you need Pantone colour separation or whether CMYK output is fine.

## Colour: The Most Common Failure Point

Colour consistency in label printing is harder to achieve than in other print categories, for three reasons:

**1. Label stock variability.** Labels print on many different substrates — white paper, clear film, metallic foil, kraft paper. The same ink behaves differently on each. A colour designed for white paper looks different on kraft.

**2. Pantone vs CMYK for brand colours.** If your brand has specific Pantone colours (defined in your brand guidelines), CMYK-only label printers will approximate them. The approximation may be 90% accurate or 70% accurate — and for premium products where shelf consistency matters, this gap is visible.

**3. Batch variation.** Even with consistent Pantone reference, different print runs can shift slightly. Premium label printers run colour proofs against your specified values. Budget printers don't.

### How to get the colour right

**Step 1:** Define your brand colours as Pantone values if you haven't. The [Pantone matching guide](/blog/pantone-matching-guide) explains how to identify and document Pantone references for your logo colours.

**Step 2:** If your printer uses digital (CMYK) output, ask for the CMYK formula that best matches your Pantone. Build your design in that CMYK value, not the RGB screen colour.

**Step 3:** Request a physical press proof or a hard-copy proof on actual label stock before approving the full run. A PDF proof on screen never shows how print colour behaves on specific materials.

**Step 4:** If colour precision is critical (luxury cosmetics, retail where shelf consistency matters), specify Pantone and use a printer who can hit it. The cost premium is real but so is the difference.

## Logo File Requirements for Labels

Regardless of printing method, your logo needs to be production-ready:

**Vector file (AI or EPS):**
- All text converted to outlines
- No embedded raster images inside the vector file
- CMYK colour mode, or Pantone swatches if spot colour printing
- All strokes expanded (not live strokes — expanded to filled paths)

**Check if your logo is truly vector** — see our post on [verifying vector files](/blog/is-my-logo-truly-vector) before submitting to any label printer.

**Minimum size on label:**
Your logo needs to be legible at its actual print size. If your label is small (a 2-inch round label, for example), test the logo at actual print size before approving. 8pt minimum font size in the logo. Fine serif details may not print cleanly at small sizes in digital printing.

**Safe zone:**
Most label printers require a minimum 0.125-inch safe margin from the label edge for content. Keep your logo inside this boundary.

## Regulatory and Compliance Considerations

For certain product categories, label content is regulated. Your logo is usually fine — the compliance burden is on ingredient lists, net weight, manufacturer details, etc. But be aware of:

**Food and beverage (FDA in the US):**
- Required fields: product name, net quantity, manufacturer name and address, ingredients list, nutrition facts
- Minimum font sizes for required elements (6pt minimum for most required text)
- Logo placement can't obscure required information

**Cosmetics (FDA / EU Cosmetics Regulation):**
- Ingredient list required (INCI names)
- Net content, responsible person information
- Country of origin for EU

**Supplements (FDA):**
- Supplement facts panel required with specific formatting
- Health claim restrictions

Your logo sits within a label that must also meet these requirements. Design the full label — not just the logo placement — before going to print.

## Finishes That Elevate Label Design

Finish choices change how your logo reads on the physical product:

**Gloss laminate:** High shine, vivid colours. Common for premium food and beverage. Logo colours appear more saturated. Downside: shows fingerprints.

**Matte laminate:** Soft, non-reflective. Perceived as premium in skincare, cosmetics, and craft products. Logo appears accurate to the design intent. Accepts pen writing (useful if the bottle needs dating).

**Soft-touch laminate:** Velvety tactile finish. Reserve for premium product lines — the cost is higher. Logo sitting on soft-touch reads as high-quality by touch alone.

**Clear label on clear material:** Logo is printed on transparent film applied to a transparent bottle. The "no-label" look. Your logo appears to be printed directly on the product. The logo must be designed to be readable against whatever the product looks like through the bottle — no relying on a white background.

**Foil elements:** Gold, silver, holographic foil applied to specific areas (logo mark, borders). Achieves premium shelf appeal. Requires separate artwork layer.
