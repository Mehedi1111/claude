Do this right now.

Open your website headline. Then open your LinkedIn bio. Then find the intro paragraph of your last proposal.

Read all three.

Do they describe the same business? Do they use the same language? Do they make the same core claim about who you help and how?

For most service businesses, the answer is no. The website was written two years ago. The LinkedIn was written by the founder in fifteen minutes. The proposal intro was copied from a previous proposal and never properly updated.

The result: your brand sounds like multiple different businesses depending on where someone finds it. Here's how to fix that properly.

---

## Why This Happens to Every Service Business

Messaging gets inconsistent for a completely predictable reason.

Different people write different channels at different times with no shared reference.

The website was written by a copywriter working from a brief. The LinkedIn was written by the founder. The proposals are written by whoever puts together the next proposal. The email newsletters are managed by whoever handles marketing this month.

Every one of these people is trying to represent the business accurately. None of them had the same source document to work from. The result: consistent intent, inconsistent output.

This isn't a discipline problem. It's a systems problem.

The fix isn't to police every piece of copy your team writes. It's to give everyone the same starting point — a written core message that adapts to each channel without losing its substance.

---

## The Three-Question Core Message

Before you can have consistent messaging across channels, you need one thing: a clear core message.

The core message answers three questions. Not four, not eight. Three.

**Who is this for?** Specific enough to be meaningful. Not "businesses" or "founders" — but "B2B professional service firms with 5–50 employees" or "independent consultants who want to move from referral-only to inbound." The specificity is what makes the message resonate with the right person and filter out the wrong one.

**What do you do for them?** The transformation or outcome, not the service description. Not "we design logos and websites" — but "we build brand identities that make professional service firms look like the category leaders they're becoming." One describes activities. The other describes results.

**Why you and not someone else?** The specific reason your positioning is different. Not "we care about quality" — every business claims that. Something real: "we're the only studio that starts with brand strategy before any design begins" or "we only work with professional service firms, which means we understand the client acquisition challenges your business actually faces."

Write this in two forms. A full version (two to three sentences). A compressed version (one sentence). These become the source from which every channel message is adapted.

If you haven't built this core message yet, [the brand messaging framework](/blog/brand-messaging-framework) walks through the full process. But you can start with these three questions today.

## The Messaging Audit: Find Your Gaps First

Before you fix anything, run a quick audit. This takes 20 minutes.

Open every primary channel where your business has a presence: website homepage, LinkedIn company page, your personal LinkedIn headline, your last proposal, your email signature.

For each one, ask four questions:

1. Is the core claim — who we're for and what we offer — clear within the first two sentences?
2. Does the language feel like it came from the same business as the website?
3. Is the tone consistent — same level of formality, same vocabulary, same sentence rhythm?
4. Are there contradictory claims (website says "boutique and specialist," LinkedIn says "full-service")?

Note every gap. You'll fix them in order of visibility — website and LinkedIn first, because those are where most prospects will encounter your brand before any personal contact.

---

## Adapting the Core Message Across Channels

The core message doesn't change. The format and length do.

**Website homepage**

The most important real estate for your core message. Your headline should express your positioning clearly and immediately. Not a clever tagline that requires interpretation — a clear statement of who you're for and what you offer.

The first paragraph should elaborate: who you serve, what you do for them, and why your approach produces better outcomes. This isn't the place to be subtle. It's the first chance to communicate the core message to a prospect who arrived from a search result and has ten seconds to decide whether to keep reading.

**LinkedIn**

LinkedIn bios have character limits and a professional audience. The core message needs to compress without losing substance.

"Brand identity and web design for B2B professional service firms. We build brand systems that make businesses look like the leaders they're becoming." That's under 150 characters. It carries the full positioning: who (B2B professional service firms), what (brand identity and web design), and the outcome (look like leaders).

Write a version that fits LinkedIn's character limit. Use it consistently across your company page About section, your personal headline, and your About paragraph. When any of these diverge — even slightly — the brand starts sounding like multiple businesses.

**Proposals**

Your proposals are a high-stakes brand touchpoint. They reach prospects who are actively deciding whether to spend significant money with you. They're often shared with other decision-makers who haven't met you.

And yet most proposals are written from scratch each time, with messaging that doesn't match the website or the LinkedIn.

The intro paragraph of every proposal should echo your core positioning. "Evoke Studio specialises in brand identity and web design for professional service businesses. Our process starts with strategy — we define your positioning before we design anything. That's why the work holds up." That's the core message, adapted for proposal context.

Keep the same language. Customise the specific solution proposed, not the description of who you are and how you work.

**Email**

Your email signature appears on every email sent. Most email signatures are a name and a phone number. A one-line descriptor — "Brand identity and web design for professional service businesses" — adds positioning to every communication automatically.

Newsletters and marketing emails should open with a sense of who they're from. A brief orientation that connects the content to the brand positioning — why this business is writing about this topic, what perspective it comes from — keeps messaging consistent even when the specific content varies.

**Social media**

The biggest consistency risk. Social media moves fast, is often managed by multiple people, and has informal norms that can pull copy toward generic content.

Two things to standardise here: the bio on each platform (should reflect the core message, adapted for character limits), and a brief written note in your messaging guide about the tone and topics that are on-brand. [How to keep your brand consistent across social profiles](/blog/brand-consistency-across-social-media) covers the platform-specific details.

## Building the Messaging Guide

This doesn't need to be complicated. A practical messaging guide for most small businesses is two to three pages.

**Core message** — the full version (two to three sentences) and the compressed version (one sentence).

**Who we serve** — specific audience description. Who the ideal client is, their situation, what they're trying to achieve, and what they've tried before.

**What we don't do** — the edges of the positioning. Everyone applying the brand should know what the business doesn't position itself as, as clearly as they know what it does.

**Channel-specific versions** — the core message adapted for: website headline, LinkedIn bio, proposal intro, email signature line. Write these out explicitly. Don't leave it to each person to adapt them independently.

**Tone description** — three to five characteristics of how the brand sounds. Direct, specific, confident. Or warm, accessible, opinionated. Or rigorous, expert, plain-spoken. This gives anyone writing content for the brand a reference for the voice.

**What to avoid** — specific language patterns that are off-brand. Buzzwords the brand doesn't use. Claims it doesn't make. Tones it doesn't adopt. These negative examples are often more useful than the positive ones.

Share it in a Google Doc or Notion page. Update it when the positioning evolves. Send it to every freelancer, agency, and new team member who creates content for the business. That's the messaging guide.

The [brand standards document](/blog/what-are-brand-standards) is the visual equivalent. The two documents together give any contributor everything they need to represent the brand accurately.

## The Test: Does It Sound Like One Business?

Here's the quick version of the audit.

Ask someone who doesn't know your business well to spend five minutes reading your website, your LinkedIn, and one proposal. Then ask them: does this sound like the same business?

If they say yes without hesitation — you're aligned.

If they pause, or say "mostly," or point out differences in how the business is described — that's your starting point.

The goal isn't identical copy across every channel. That would read as stiff and robotic. The goal is the same core story, told in formats appropriate to each context.

The website version is more formal and detailed. The LinkedIn is compressed. The proposal is specific to the engagement. The email is conversational. But underneath all of it, the same positioning. The same audience. The same core claim about what makes the business the right choice.

That's brand messaging consistency. Not a single voice everywhere — a single story, told in the right register for each room you walk into.
