How long does brand identity design take? It's a fair question, and the honest answer is: it depends on what you're actually building.

"Brand identity" means different things to different agencies. A logo and a colour choice is a brand identity. So is a comprehensive 80-page brand system covering positioning, messaging, visual guidelines, and applications across 12 touchpoints. These take very different amounts of time.

Here's a realistic breakdown.

---

## How Long Does Logo Design Take?

A logo-only project — one logo mark, wordmark, or combination of both — typically takes **2–4 weeks** from project start to final delivery.

The stages in a standard logo project:

**Week 1: Discovery and briefing** — the designer learns about your business, your audience, your competitors, and the feeling the logo needs to convey. A thorough brief here prevents wasted directions later.

**Week 1–2: Concept development** — the designer explores directions internally, narrows them down, and prepares 2–3 distinct concepts to present. This is where the time investment is highest and least visible to you.

**Week 2–3: Concept presentation and feedback** — you review concepts, share feedback, choose a direction. One round of revisions refines the chosen concept based on your input.

**Week 3–4: Finalisation and delivery** — the logo is refined, all variants are produced (colour versions, reversed, mono), files are packaged and delivered.

Projects take less time when your feedback is clear and comes quickly. They take more time when you need additional revision rounds or when the first concept round doesn't land and needs rethinking.

---

## How Long Does a Full Brand Identity Take?

A complete brand identity — logo, colour palette, typography system, brand guidelines, and application design — typically takes **4–8 weeks**.

The additional time goes into:

**Deeper discovery** — understanding your positioning, your audience in detail, your competitive landscape. This usually involves a formal brand workshop or multiple discovery sessions.

**Brand strategy development** — defining your positioning, personality, and messaging before any visual design begins. This stage doesn't produce visible outputs yet, which can feel slow. But it's the stage that ensures the visual work that follows is making deliberate decisions rather than aesthetic ones.

**Extended concept development** — more directions to explore, more time per direction, more strategic rationale required for each choice.

**Application design** — showing how the brand looks on business cards, social media, signage, email headers, presentations. This makes the brand real and tests whether it holds up across different surfaces.

**Brand guidelines production** — documenting the rules for how the brand is used, so that everyone who touches it in the future does so consistently. [What brand guidelines should include](/blog/brand-guidelines-what-they-include) covers everything a proper style guide documents.

A 4-week project is usually a simpler brand with a lean process. 8 weeks is more typical for a thorough job at a professional agency.

---

## How Long Does a Rebrand Take?

Rebranding an existing business adds complexity that a new brand doesn't have.

There's existing brand equity to assess — what parts of the current brand are known and valuable, what parts need to go, what needs to evolve rather than change. There are existing assets to audit: every place the current brand appears that will need updating after the rebrand. And there's a rollout to plan: you can't rebrand silently if you have an existing audience.

A rebrand typically takes **6–12 weeks** for the brand identity work itself, before rollout begins. Rollout — updating your website, social channels, printed materials, signage, and all other brand touchpoints — is a separate project that runs after. For context on what rebrand projects involve and what they cost, [the rebranding cost guide](/blog/rebranding-cost-guide) covers the full scope.

## What Makes a Branding Project Take Longer?

Understanding the delay factors helps you avoid them.

**Slow client feedback.** Agencies work in stages. Each stage requires your sign-off before the next can start. A 48-hour turnaround on feedback keeps a project moving. A 2-week delay on feedback adds 2 weeks to your timeline — it's that direct.

**Unclear brief.** A vague brief produces vague concepts. When the concepts don't land, the project goes back to the beginning. A thorough brief — specific audience, specific feeling, specific competitors — produces more targeted first concepts and fewer wasted rounds. [How to brief a logo designer](/blog/how-to-brief-a-logo-designer) covers what makes a brief actually useful.

**Too many decision-makers.** Projects with a single decision-maker move faster than projects that require committee approval at every stage. If three people have veto power and they disagree with each other, the project can stall almost indefinitely. Agree on your decision-making process before the project starts.

**Scope expansion.** The project starts as a logo, then someone adds "can we also do business cards, a brand pattern, social media templates, and a pitch deck?" mid-project. Each addition extends the timeline. Scope changes aren't wrong — but they need to be acknowledged as timeline additions, not additions that somehow don't affect the schedule.

**Revision rounds beyond what was scoped.** Most projects include 2 revision rounds. When a project needs 4 or 5, either the brief wasn't clear, the concept selection wasn't decisive, or the feedback is contradictory. All three are fixable — but all three add time.

## What Can Be Done Faster Without Cutting Quality?

Some elements of a branding project can be accelerated without compromising the result.

**Discovery can be compressed** if your brief is excellent and you arrive at the first call with deep clarity on your audience, positioning, and vision. A designer who would normally spend a week in discovery can move faster with a client who's done the thinking already.

**Concept rounds can be reduced** if you give highly specific, actionable feedback. "Explore something more geometric and less illustrative" is actionable. "I'm not sure, it just doesn't feel right" is not — and leads to another round of guessing.

**File delivery can be expedited** if you confirm requirements upfront. Knowing exactly what file formats, what sizes, and what variants you need means the designer prepares them correctly the first time rather than going back and forth.

Rush fees are standard in the industry for projects that need to compress a 6-week schedule into 3. If you have a hard launch date, communicate it clearly upfront and ask whether it's achievable before you start.

---

## What Timeline Should You Give a New Agency?

If you're planning a product launch, website redesign, or any event that depends on having your brand ready, work backwards.

Give the agency 6 weeks minimum for a complete brand identity. Add 2 weeks buffer for any dependencies (website build starts after brand delivery, for example). Add another week for any print production with lead times.

A launch in October means your brand project should start by mid-August. A startup that comes to an agency 3 weeks before launch is setting itself up for a rushed result or a delayed launch.
