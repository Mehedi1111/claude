Nike's swoosh is always the same size relative to the wordmark. Always the same Pantone colour. Always the same amount of clear space around it.

This doesn't happen by accident. Someone wrote it down. A document exists that specifies exactly how the swoosh is used — and what never to do with it. Every agency, supplier, and employee uses that document.

That's brand standards.

You need them. Not because you're Nike. Because anyone other than you is going to make their own decisions about your brand if you don't give them something better to work from.

---

## What Happens Without Brand Standards

Here's what brand inconsistency looks like in practice.

Your marketing agency uses a slightly different shade of your brand blue because they approximated it from looking at your website — not from a hex code you gave them.

Your new social media manager posts graphics using a font that looks similar to your brand font but isn't it — because nobody told them which font to use.

Your print supplier reproduces your logo at a slightly different scale on merchandise because the file you sent them was a JPEG and they re-created the proportions by eye.

Six months later your brand looks like four different businesses depending on where someone finds it.

None of these people made a bad decision. They made the best decision they could with the information they had. The problem isn't the people. The problem is the absence of documented standards that would have given them the right answer.

Brand standards fix this. Not by policing every use of the brand, but by making the right use easy enough to follow that people don't have to guess.

## What Brand Standards Cover

Brand standards live in a document — called a style guide, brand guidelines document, or brand book — and cover these core areas.

### Logo Usage

This is the most detailed section of most brand standards documents.

**Logo versions.** Your brand probably has more than one logo version. A full combination mark (symbol plus wordmark). A wordmark only. A symbol only. Standards specify when each version is appropriate and when it isn't.

**Minimum size.** Below a certain size your logo becomes unreadable. Standards define the minimum — usually in millimetres for print and pixels for digital. Below this size, you use a simplified version.

**Clear space.** The minimum breathing room around the logo. A zone that should contain no other visual element. Usually defined as a multiple of a key element in the logo — the cap height of the wordmark, or the width of a specific shape. This prevents the logo from being crowded by text or other graphics in a way that makes it harder to read.

**Colour on backgrounds.** Which logo version works on which background. Full colour on white or light backgrounds. Reversed white version on dark backgrounds. Mono on mid-tones. This guidance means anyone applying your logo doesn't have to figure it out — they just follow the rule.

**What not to do.** A section showing incorrect logo usage: stretching it, changing the colours, adding effects, placing it on a background it doesn't work against, using an old version. Seeing what's wrong is often more useful than seeing what's right.

### Colour Palette

Your brand colours with exact values for every medium.

**HEX** for web and digital.
**RGB** for screen use.
**CMYK** for print reproduction.
**Pantone** for brand-critical print — packaging, signage, quality stationery — where colour accuracy matters more than approximation.

Standards also specify approved colour combinations. Which colours work together. Which ones to avoid. What text colour is approved on each background. This prevents someone making a colour decision that's technically on-brand in isolation but looks wrong in context.

For reference on where these colour rules matter most in daily practice, [brand consistency across social media](/blog/brand-consistency-across-social-media) covers the specific applications.

### Typography

Your brand fonts and how to use them.

**Primary typeface.** Your main font. Used for headlines, display text, the most prominent typography in your materials.

**Secondary typeface.** For body text and supporting copy. Typically more readable at smaller sizes.

**Hierarchy.** The relative sizes and weights at each level — H1, H2, H3, body, caption, label. This keeps visual rhythm consistent across all materials.

**Approved weights.** Which font weights (Bold, SemiBold, Regular, Light) are approved for which uses. Using a font in too many weights in the same design creates visual noise.

**Web fallback fonts.** What to use when your brand font isn't available in a web context or isn't installed on a recipient's system.

### Tone of Voice

How the brand communicates in writing.

Not every brand standards document includes tone of voice — some treat this as a separate brand messaging document. But at minimum, standards should describe: is the brand formal or conversational? Direct or nurturing? Expert or approachable? Does it use contractions? Does it use humour?

Tone of voice guidance prevents the brand from sounding like different people depending on who's writing that week. The brand voice should be recognisable whether it's a website headline, an email subject line, or a social media post.

### Imagery Style

How photography and illustration are used.

For brands where imagery is prominent, this section defines the visual approach. Types of shots — lifestyle, product, editorial, environmental. Colour treatment — bright and airy, moody and high-contrast, warm and saturated. What the brand's visual world looks like. What it doesn't look like.

For brands where imagery is less central, this can be a brief note on the general direction rather than a comprehensive photography guide.

## What Brand Standards Prevent

The value isn't abstract. Here's what they prevent concretely.

**The outdated logo problem.** Without standards specifying which version is current, suppliers and employees use old versions long after a rebrand. A document with a "last updated" date and the current approved files ends this.

**The wrong shade of blue.** Without a CMYK or Pantone value, "our brand colour is blue" means something slightly different to every printer. Some jobs come back subtly wrong. Standards give suppliers the exact values they need.

**The freelancer who goes off-brand.** When a new social media manager or copywriter joins, they make decisions based on what they think looks right. Without a standards document, those decisions are guesses. With one, they have the rules.

**The stretched logo.** Someone opens the logo PNG in a presentation tool, drags a corner, forgets to hold Shift. The logo is now distorted. A standards document that specifies minimum sizes and correct file formats to use — and includes the actual files — prevents this. Or at least makes it clear when it's wrong.

**The inconsistent proposal.** A new team member puts together a client proposal using their own font choices and a logo they pulled from the website. It looks almost right. But "almost right" across multiple proposals means the firm looks different every time it sends one.

## How Formal Do Brand Standards Need to Be?

The size and formality of a brand standards document should match the complexity of the brand and the number of people using it.

**A 4–8 page PDF** covering logo versions and usage rules, colour palette with exact values, typography specification, and a brief tone of voice summary. That's enough for a small business with a solo team. Clean, practical, easy to follow.

**A 12–20 page PDF** adds imagery style guidelines, application examples — business card, email signature, social templates, document headers — and more detailed tone of voice with examples and common mistakes.

**A full brand book (30+ pages)** covers everything above plus detailed application design across every touchpoint, sub-brand guidance, co-branding rules, and usage rules for specific contexts like events, merchandise, or digital advertising.

A solo consultant needs a 4-page reference. A firm with 20 employees and multiple freelancers using the brand independently needs something more comprehensive.

Most professional branding packages include brand standards as a deliverable. If yours didn't — or if your current document is too vague to be useful — [what to include in a branding package](/blog/what-is-a-branding-package) covers what a complete delivery looks like and how to ask for what's missing.

## Creating Brand Standards If You Don't Have Them

If you have a brand but no standards document, here's how to start. It doesn't need to be designed beautifully. A clear Word document is better than a beautifully designed PDF that nobody can find.

**Step 1: Gather what you have.** Collect your logo files (all versions), your colour codes, your font names and weights. If these aren't documented anywhere, your original designer should have them.

**Step 2: Document the basics.** Write down: your exact brand name spelling. Your logo versions and when to use each. Your colour codes in HEX (add CMYK if you print). Your font names and where each is used.

**Step 3: Add the rules.** Minimum logo size. Clear space requirements. Which logo version works on which background. What not to do.

**Step 4: Write a brief tone guide.** Three sentences: what tone the brand uses, what it avoids, one example of the brand in a sentence.

**Step 5: Make it accessible.** Share it with everyone who will ever use your brand. Save it somewhere with a stable, shareable link — Google Drive or Dropbox. Send it to any freelancer before they start. Include it in your onboarding materials for new team members.

The document you create this week is better than the document you never create because you were waiting for the perfect version.

---

## Keeping Standards Current

Brand standards are only useful if they're up to date.

Update your standards document after any significant brand change — new logo, updated colours, rebrand. When you make a smaller update — adding a secondary colour, approving a new font for a specific use — update the document immediately and tell anyone currently using it.

Date the document. "Last updated: [Month] [Year]" in the header or footer means anyone looking at it knows whether it reflects the current brand.

Version control matters. If your standards document has gone through multiple versions, keep only the current one in the shared folder. Old versions create confusion about which one is right.

The [brand framework](/blog/what-is-a-brand-framework) is the strategic document that sits behind the standards — the thinking that explains why the brand looks and sounds the way it does. The standards implement the framework. Both matter. The framework tells you what the brand should be. The standards tell everyone how to use it correctly.
