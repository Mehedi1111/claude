What are brand standards? They're the rules that keep your brand looking and sounding like itself — regardless of who's applying it, on what platform, or in what context.

Think about Nike. The swoosh is always the same size relative to the wordmark. The typeface is always the same. The colour is always the same Pantone. None of this happens by accident. It happens because someone documented exactly how the brand is used — and every agency, supplier, and employee follows those rules.

That's brand standards. And you need them even if you're not Nike.

## What Brand Standards Cover

Brand standards typically live in a document — called a brand guidelines document, brand book, or style guide — and cover these core areas.

### Logo Usage Rules

How the logo is used correctly — and how it isn't.

**Primary logo vs. alternative versions.** Your brand probably has more than one logo version: a full combination mark (symbol plus wordmark), a wordmark only, and a symbol only. The standards specify when each version is appropriate. The full mark for primary use. The symbol alone for app icons and small profile photos. The wordmark for situations where the symbol isn't needed.

**Minimum size.** Below a certain size, your logo becomes illegible. Brand standards define the minimum size for print (usually in millimetres) and for digital (in pixels). Smaller than this, use a simplified version.

**Clear space.** The minimum breathing room around the logo — the zone that shouldn't contain any other visual element. This is usually defined as a multiple of a specific element in the logo (often the cap height of the wordmark or the width of a key shape).

**Colour on backgrounds.** Which logo version works on which background colour. Full colour on white. Reversed white on dark backgrounds. Mono on mid-tones. Standards specify this so nobody has to guess.

**What not to do.** Specific examples of incorrect usage: stretching the logo, changing the colours, adding a drop shadow, placing it on a background it doesn't work against, using an outdated version. Seeing what's wrong is often more useful than seeing what's right.

### Colour Palette

Your approved brand colours with exact values for every medium.

**Primary colours** — the core palette used most often. Typically 2–3 colours.

**Secondary colours** — supporting colours used for variety in design, backgrounds, infographics, and supporting elements.

**Neutral colours** — whites, off-whites, light greys, dark greys. These are the colours used for backgrounds and large areas of the design without being the "brand colour."

**Values for every medium:**
- HEX for web and digital
- RGB for screen use
- CMYK for print
- Pantone for precision print production

Standards also specify approved colour combinations — which colours can be used together, which can't, and what text colours work on each background. This prevents someone choosing a combination that's on-brand in isolation but looks wrong in context. The [brand consistency across social media](/blog/brand-consistency-across-social-media) guide shows where these colour rules matter most in everyday digital use.

### Typography

Your brand fonts and how to use them.

**Primary typeface** — your main font, used for headlines, display text, and the most prominent typography in your materials.

**Secondary typeface** — for body text, supporting copy, and smaller text contexts.

**Hierarchy** — the relative sizes and weights used at each level: H1, H2, H3, body, caption, label. This keeps visual rhythm consistent across all materials.

**Web fallback fonts** — what to use when your primary brand font isn't available in a web context.

**Approved weights** — which font weights (Bold, SemiBold, Regular, Light) are approved for which uses. This matters because using a font in too many weights in the same design creates visual noise.

### Tone of Voice

How the brand communicates in words.

Not every brand standards document includes tone of voice — some treat this as a separate brand messaging document. But the basics should be present: is the brand formal or conversational? Direct or nurturing? Expert or approachable? Does it use humour or maintain a serious tone?

Tone of voice standards also cover practical writing rules: sentence length preferences, whether the brand uses contractions (we're vs. we are), how it handles technical language, and what to avoid (jargon, buzzwords, overpromising language).

### Imagery Style

How photography and illustration are used.

For brands where imagery is prominent, this section defines the visual approach: the types of shots used (lifestyle, product, editorial, architectural), the colour treatment (bright and clean, moody and dark, warm and earthy), the types of subjects and scenes that are on-brand, and what to avoid.

For brands where imagery is less central, this can be a brief note on the general direction rather than a comprehensive photography guide.

## What Brand Standards Actually Prevent

The value of brand standards isn't abstract. Here's what they prevent concretely.

**The outdated logo problem.** Without standards specifying which logo version is current, suppliers and employees continue using old versions long after a rebrand. A unified standards document with a "last updated" date eliminates this.

**The wrong shade of blue.** Without a Pantone or CMYK value, "our brand colour is blue" means something slightly different to every printer. Some jobs come back subtly wrong. Standards give suppliers the exact values they need.

**The freelancer who goes off-brand.** When a new social media manager or copywriter joins, they make aesthetic decisions based on what they think looks right. Without a standards document to reference, those decisions are guesses. With one, they have the rules to follow.

**The inconsistent LinkedIn presence.** Without guidance on tone, the company LinkedIn sounds different from the website, which sounds different from the newsletter. Standards align the written voice across every channel.

**The expanded logo.** Someone opens the logo PNG in a presentation tool, drags a corner to make it bigger, doesn't hold Shift, and now the logo is distorted. Standards with minimum sizes and correct file formats prevent this — or at least make it clear when it's wrong.

## What Brand Standards Look Like in Practice

For small businesses, brand standards don't need to be a 100-page document. They need to be a usable reference.

**A 4–8 page PDF** covering: logo versions and usage rules, colour palette with values, typography specification, tone of voice summary. That's enough for a business with a small team and straightforward brand needs.

**A 12–20 page PDF** adds: imagery style, application examples (business card, email signature, social templates), expanded tone of voice with examples, do's and don'ts for each brand element.

**A full brand book (20+ pages)** includes all of the above plus detailed application design across every touchpoint, sub-brand guidance, and co-branding rules.

The size should match the complexity of the brand and the number of people using it. A solo consultant needs a 4-page reference. A firm with 12 employees and multiple freelancers using the brand independently needs something more comprehensive.

Most professional branding packages include brand standards as a deliverable. If yours didn't, [what to include in a branding package](/blog/what-is-a-branding-package) covers what a complete delivery looks like — and how to ask for what's missing.

## How to Create Brand Standards for Your Business

If you don't have a standards document yet, here's where to start.

**Gather what you have.** Collect your logo files (all versions), your colour codes, your font names and weights. If these aren't documented anywhere, your designer should have them.

**Document the basics.** Create a simple document: logo versions with usage notes, colour palette with HEX values (add CMYK if you print), font names and where each is used.

**Add the rules.** Minimum logo size, clear space requirement, which logo version works on which background, what not to do.

**Write a brief tone guide.** Three sentences: what tone the brand uses, what it avoids, one example of the brand voice in a sentence.

**Make it accessible.** Share it with everyone who will ever use your brand — save it in a shared folder, send it to suppliers, give it to any new freelancer before they start work.

It doesn't have to be designed beautifully. A clear Word document is better than a beautifully designed PDF that nobody can find.
